# Implementation plan

**Status: the sequence and the gates are settled. Each step's internals are not — that is what per-step design produces.**

[roadmap.md](roadmap.md) draws the scope line and names four milestones. This document is the next resolution down: **thirty-six steps, each one independently designable, buildable and testable**, in an order where every step's dependencies are behind it.

A step is not a task list. It is a *unit of design*: small enough that its open questions can be closed in one sitting, self-contained enough that it can be tested without the steps after it, and shaped so that finishing it leaves the system in a working state rather than a half-migrated one. The details are deliberately absent — each step gets its own design pass when it comes up, and the **Refs** line says which numbered sections that pass starts from.

## 1. How to read this

Each step carries:

- **Delivers** — the capability, in a sentence.
- **Scope** — the two to five things the design pass has to settle. Not exhaustive.
- **Refs** — numbered sections in the design docs. `exec §7.3` means [execution.md](execution.md) section 7.3.
- **Done when** — the observable condition that lets the next step start. Almost always a test, because almost everything here is testable without a human.

Three markers appear:

- ⚠ **upstream N** — blocked or degraded pending item N of [execution.md](execution.md) §12. The step says whether a workaround exists. A few steps depend on a change in a *frontend* rather than in Inspect; those say so.
- 🔧 — **test infrastructure** rather than a user-visible feature. Two steps are largely infrastructure, and both are placed earlier than they look like they belong.
- ◐ **partial** — part of the step shipped because a later one needed it, and the rest is deferred rather than forgotten. The step names both halves.

Gates **M2**, **M3**, **M4** are [roadmap.md](roadmap.md) §3's milestones, located precisely. **Ship** is not pinned to one of them here; the only thing this document fixes about it is that it falls before §8.

Every step's design pass also has a **test budget** to respect — see §10, which is measured rather than guessed and is the one constraint that binds across all thirty-six steps at once.

## 2. Foundations — nothing spawns a process yet

Five steps that establish state, identity, and the decision function. None of them builds process machinery, which is why they are first: the component most likely to be subtly wrong is the cheapest one here to test exhaustively, and debugging it through a live fleet would be miserable.

### Step 1 — Identifier correlation ✅ **done**

**Delivered** the verified assumption everything downstream inherits: a landed `.eval` maps back to a manifest task. `tests/evalset/test_selection.py` — originally five cases offline plus one behind `network`, eleven process launches, 33s serial and under 10s with `-n auto` (§10). Step 6 took two of them: the concurrent fleet and the resume were the production shape, and they belong on the production spawn. What is left here is the case production never has — one worker running a whole fifteen-task manifest.

The property holds. What sharpened during the work is *which half* needed verifying: a worker matches its selection by recomputing identifiers from its own resolved tasks, so a selection that runs at all already proves capture↔resolve agreement across processes. The unexercised half is resolve↔**log** — the half `reconcile` reads — and upstream touches it only when validating a resume target.

The fixtures are built so a field that silently dropped out of the hash would show up as a **collision** rather than a vacuous pass: fifteen tasks sharing one name and one args set, each differing in exactly one identity-relevant field (args, version, model, model args, model roles, solver chain, generate config, and every execution limit including the `output:<n>` token encoding). Fifteen shapes, fifteen distinct identifiers, fifteen logs, exact match. A second fixture covers the eval-set-level args, where the interesting case is a task whose own limits are *shadowed* by the eval set's — capture merges them into the hash while the log reads `eval.config`, and that merge round-trips.

Three findings went back into the design:

- **`identifier_version` was being dropped.** `EvalSetCapture` carries it; `read_eval_set()` discarded it. A manifest is committed as desired state and outlives an inspect upgrade, so a `TASK_IDENTIFIER_VERSION` bump would have made a finished sweep read as unstarted. Now carried (config §4); refusing on it is step 5.
- **A selection's `log_dir` does not reach pre-boundary writes** (exec §4). A flow worker given only the override drops `flow.yaml` into the definition's log directory. A worker needs both channels — step 6.
- **cwd does not break correlation**, contrary to the hazard upstream's error text warns about, because `definition_command` resolves the definition absolutely and flow anchors task files to the spec. Pinned as a test — which now runs against the real spawn, where losing it would matter.

### Step 2 — Workspace and `init` ✅ **done**

**Delivered** a real `steward init`, plus `Workspace` — the one place the layout is expressed, so no later step builds a path from a string. `tests/workspace/`, 19 cases, 4s, no subprocesses but `git init`.

Two decisions taken during the work:

- **The journal marks the workspace, and `init` opens it with a real `initialized` event.** Nothing else could mark it: `.steward/` is disposable, a definition can sit anywhere, and `_steward.yaml` is optional (step 12). This pulls a minimal envelope and append path forward from step 3, which keeps the fold and the vocabulary where they belong, and it gives `Workspace.find()` something to walk up for (workflow §5.1).
- **The definition placeholder is empty**, and `--type` chooses only its filename — including `hawk.yaml`, since with nothing being authored there was no reason to exclude it. workflow §5's promise of a runnable scaffold was amended rather than left contradicting the code; what a good starting point contains is deferred.

A **skeletal `steward runbook`** ships too, so `AGENTS.md` can take its final shape now instead of naming a command that errors. Not a stub: agent.md §6's prohibitions and §5/§9's reading disciplines are settled, so it carries those for real and marks the operational sections *not yet written*.

One test defect found and fixed: the pre-existing `test_cli_init` invoked `init` with no directory, which wrote a workspace into the repository and appended to its `.gitignore`. `init` was right; the test was not.

### Step 3 — The journal ✅ **done**

**Delivered** the append-only record as something that can be read after a crash: a safe concurrent append, a damage-tolerant reader, and `summarize()` — the first fold, and the shape every later one takes. `tests/workspace/test_journal.py`, 14 cases, 2.2s.

Three decisions, each the opposite of how Steward treats a selection document — and deliberately, because **a selection is input being validated before it changes what runs, while a journal is history being read**:

- **An unrecognised event type reads as a generic event.** A workspace outlives the Steward that wrote it, so refusing a file because a later version put something new in it would be the wrong trade. Selection documents forbid extras for exactly the opposite reason.
- **Damage costs one line, never the file.** `read_journal` returns what it parsed *and* what it could not, with line numbers; a missing journal is an empty history rather than damage. Nothing raises, and nothing is swallowed — where a complaint goes is `steward.log`, step 22.
- **Only `initialized` is typed.** The other eight types in workflow §5.6 arrive with the steps that write them; in particular the five anomaly types stay with step 23, which keeps the three-level model as one piece rather than transcribing a table ahead of the code that gives it meaning.

One thing measured rather than assumed, because the first version of the test could not have failed: **splitting a record across two writes** (payload, then newline) loses about a quarter of the events under four concurrent writers. Size is not the hazard and neither is the platform — a buffered whole-line append is safe on a local filesystem. So the guard is one `os.write` of a pre-built line, and the test's docstring records what it does and does not catch.

### Step 4 — Observed state, and the fixtures that prove it 🔧 ✅ **done**

**Delivered** the read half of convergence — `observe_logs` turns a log directory into attempts grouped by identifier, `observe_tasks` reads those against a manifest — and `tests/_logs.py`, which synthesizes such a directory without running anything. `tests/evalset/test_observe.py`, 23 cases, 1.2s, **zero process launches**.

Four decisions:

- **The split is at the filesystem boundary, not at the manifest.** `observe_logs` does the I/O and knows nothing about what was supposed to run, which is what lets it serve `logs-archive/` and the flow store — neither of which has a manifest to compare against. Completeness is a second, pure function, so step 5's inputs stay pure.
- **Four states, one carrying a reason.** `complete`, `incomplete`, `missing`, `orphaned` — deliberately the domain of the action vocabulary rather than a taxonomy of log conditions. Every incomplete task takes the same action, so *why* (`started`, `short`, `invalidated`, `error`, `cancelled`, `no_results`) is reporting material. Complete-clean and complete-with-errors are one state and a count, for the same reason: both mean *do not spawn*, and the errored samples are step 23's queue.
- **Attempts order by `eval.created`, and the latest *successful* one is current.** Both halves diverge from upstream, which sorts by mtime and takes the newest whatever its status. Mtime is not intrinsic — restoring a log from the archive rewrites it, and the archive is a cache the design intends to hit — while `created` survives even the mid-run header fallback. And a deliberate re-run that errored must not displace a good result (exec §5.8).
- **An unreadable log costs one log, never the directory** — step 3's rule, applied to the other thing Steward reads on a schedule. Not hypothetical: a worker's zip has no readable header for the moment between creation and its first journal entry, and a tend that raised on that is a tend that never ran.

Two things measured or read rather than assumed. **Header reads are concurrent**, because an `.eval` header read genuinely awaits on I/O where a `json` one is synchronous inside its `async def` — so the fixtures can prove the reader right and can say nothing about its speed, and a local benchmark over them would argue for exactly the wrong thing. And **the mid-run `.eval` case is layer 1 after all**: a zip with one member, `_journal/start.json`, reproduces it in ten lines.

Two findings, both recorded upstream (exec §12, items 6 and 11): `read_eval_log_headers_async` raises on any single unreadable file, so a scheduled reader cannot use it; and the capture manifest discards the epochs *reducer*, so a reducer-only change reads as complete where `eval_set()` would re-score. A third went to workflow §2.1 — **renaming the definition file orphans the entire run**, since `task_file` is in the identifier, and the workspace's fixed definition name is what makes Steward immune.

**Generator and reader were one step because they are mutually defining.** Neither is testable alone. What made the generator trustworthy is that one `_eval_spec()` builds the `EvalSpec` both the manifest row and every log derive from, so they agree on the identifier by construction rather than by a literal repeated twice.

### Step 5 — `reconcile` ✅ **done**

**Delivered** the decision function — `(manifest, inflight, observed, pool, paused) -> (actions, queued, summary)`, pure. `tests/schedule/`, 29 cases, 1.4s, no process ever started.

Four decisions:

- **The state enum *is* the action vocabulary.** Step 4's four states map one to one onto what to do — `complete` leave it, `incomplete` resume it, `missing` spawn it, `orphaned` report it — so `reconcile` has no classification logic of its own. Every incomplete task resumes whatever went wrong; there is deliberately no branch on the reason, because resume reuses exactly the samples worth keeping.
- **A manifest from a different inspect raises rather than reports.** Unmatchable identifiers make every task read *missing* and every log read *orphaned*, so a finished sweep would re-run from scratch — and a summary carrying that looks entirely normal. A returned flag asks every future consumer to remember to check it; an exception cannot be forgotten. Step 13's `tend` catches it in one place and says `steward launch`.
- **`archive` is not in the vocabulary yet.** Orphans are named in the summary and nothing acts on them. An action nobody can execute is a stub, and a tend computing twelve archive actions every ten minutes and running none of them is noise. It arrives with the launch gate (step 16) and signoff's sweep (step 26).
- **The queue holds `SpawnWorker`s, not identifiers** — the same decision deferred, so *approved re-runs go first* (step 25) becomes a sort rather than a second code path.

The crash-recovery case is the one worth naming: **a live worker and one that died mid-run leave exactly the same thing in the log directory** — a `started` log with no results. Only the in-flight record separates them, which is why `reconcile` takes it, and getting it wrong means either double-spawning a live task or never recovering a dead one. It has its own test.

Two things changed in the design while building it.

**`max_samples` was not in the capture manifest**, so scheduling.md's *yield to whatever the definition set* silently could not happen — a definition asking for 60 got Steward's 40 and nobody was told, and the log records only the effective value so there was no read-side workaround. Fixed upstream: `options` now carries `max_samples`, no schema bump needed since `options` is a free-form dict.

Reading it exposed a second thing, which is why `resolve_max_samples` has **three** sources rather than two: Steward's default and an operator's explicit instruction are not the same claim. Collapsing them means either the fallback outranks a definition or a typed `--max-samples` loses to one, and neither shows up in the resulting number. So `Pool.max_samples` is `int | None`, where `None` means *no preference*, and the order is operator → definition → 40.

**The ceiling stopped being derived from cores.** §2.2's argument — *past core count another process buys no parallelism* — misreads the workload: a worker is on the CPU in bursts and waiting on a model API in between, so ten workers on four cores is ordinary. One process per task buys isolation of those bursts, not core saturation, which makes the ceiling a resource guard rather than a parallelism budget. It is now a flat **10**, matching where `eval_set()`'s own `max_tasks` starts, and expected to be raised. That deleted `available_cores()` and its cgroup reading — the cgroup lie is still real, but it now surfaces only inside Docker's `default_concurrency()` at step 27, where §3.6 records it. Recoverable from `f1e1822` if that step wants it. The formula also needed the running-workers term: `min(ceiling − running, pending)`, since eight running and five pending under a ceiling of ten is two spawns, not five.

Sandbox division is *not* here; it is step 27, blocked upstream. `reconcile` divides one budget at this step and grows a second later.

## 3. Execution — processes, and the machinery that survives them

### Step 6 — Worker spawn ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `Fleet.spawn(action)` — a `SpawnWorker` becomes a selection document and a detached process, and the log it lands recomputes to the identifier that asked for it. `tests/worker/test_spawn.py`, 8 cases, 13s, 12 process launches; half of them need none.

The loop now closes end to end: capture → reconcile → spawn → land → reconcile, and the second reconcile returns no actions.

Four decisions:

- **`spawn` builds its own command.** `log_dir` has to reach both the frontend, which writes before `eval_set()` is ever called, and the selection, which reaches the boundary — and a caller that passes one and forgets the other gets a flow worker dropping `flow.yaml` into the definition's log directory (step 1 measured it). Taking a prepared `DefinitionCommand` would leave that gap open forever; building it inside is what makes the two channels one parameter. `max_samples` needs only the selection: nothing before the boundary runs samples.
- **Each worker's output goes to a file** beside its selection, merged and appended. `DEVNULL` is what a Hawk worker dying in `uv pip install` would leave behind, and `PIPE` is a deadlock waiting for a reader who has exited. It is the only account of the pre-boundary window (exec §7.3) and it is cheap — a `plain` display leaves twenty legible lines per worker.
- **The handle carries its `Popen`.** Detached is not reparented: `start_new_session` detaches from the terminal, and a worker stays its spawner's child until the spawner exits. Dropping the reference is worse than keeping it — `Popen.__del__` files the child in `subprocess._active`, where the next `Popen()` reaps it, so a later `waitpid` fails intermittently. The field is documented as valid only while this process lives; step 8's record is what survives.
- **The eval set id is resolved once, by the caller.** `resolve_eval_set_id` is a one-line delegation to upstream's `eval_set_id_for_log_dir`, which reads-or-mints-and-writes `.eval-set-id` idempotently and refuses a conflicting id. Worker mode never touches that file (exec §4.1), so keeping it out of `spawn` is what keeps *once, at run start* a visible constraint rather than an accident.

Three things read rather than assumed. **The control server needs nothing at spawn**: it is on by default and binds a per-pid socket, so N workers cannot collide, and `INSPECT_EVAL_CTL_SERVER` is a CLI envvar that a definition script never consults — no ambient environment can switch a worker's control surface off. **A worker prints no launch handoff**, because the handoff and the ctl pointer are armed by the CLI; Steward knows the pid because it spawned it. And **`INSPECT_EVAL_SET_CAPTURE` must be stripped**, not merely left unset — capture and selection are mutually exclusive, so a worker inheriting an exported capture path would die at startup instead of running.

Two corrections to execution.md. **§7.3's "passes that path in argv" is not implementable**: argv is Steward's to compose only for a raw script, and a Flow or Hawk worker is that platform's CLI, where an extra positional argument is a parse error — exactly the two types whose pre-boundary window is long enough to need self-identification. The marker is the environment (`INSPECT_EVAL_SET_SELECTION` already *is* the path), read with `psutil.Process.environ()`, verified readable for a same-user detached child. And **§7.1's `exited` row overstated the exit status**: a tend exits in seconds while its workers run for hours, so by the time anything observes one gone it has been reparented and its status reaped by init. *Observed gone* is what the record can promise, which is why the reap action carries nothing else.

One decision the step forced: **Steward is POSIX-only, declined rather than deferred.** `start_new_session` is silently ignored on Windows, so a worker there stays attached to its console and dies with it — the central guarantee failing quietly. A port needs `creationflags` plus a second story for AF_UNIX control sockets, `getsid`, process-table identification, and the signals step 11 sends, which is a second execution model rather than a flag. Declared in the package metadata and refused at the spawn, because a classifier is metadata `pip` does not enforce and a platform that appears to work is worse than one that does not.

One finding for later: **a definition declaring a scanner cannot be run by Steward today** — worker mode rejects scanners outright (exec §4.2), so the worker exits with an upstream `PrerequisiteError`. The capture manifest records `scanners` in `options`, so `launch` can refuse early with a better message. *Done at step 16* — the refusal happens between the capture and the commit, so nothing is written and no worker starts. Answered properly at step 28.

### Step 7 — Once-per-run pre-boundary work ✅ **done**

**Delivered** a flow fan-out that keeps its once-per-run work out of the run's log directory, and an accurate account of what Hawk's fan-out actually costs. `tests/worker/test_flow_worker.py`, one case, three process launches.

**The skip mechanism the step was scoped around was the wrong thing to reach for**, because a frontend change is not available: Steward runs `inspect_flow` and `hawk` as released. What *is* available is aiming the work somewhere harmless with controls both already ship — and for Flow that reaches two thirds of it, using nothing but `flow run` options.

**Every flow worker gets a scratch directory of its own** (`.steward/workers/<stem>/`) through the frontend `--log-dir` channel, while the selection carries the run's. All three pre-boundary items key off `spec.log_dir`, so `flow.yaml` and the requirements snapshot stop being N concurrent writes to two shared paths, and `find_existing_logs` scans an empty directory instead of one that grows all run. Safe because the scan's result never reaches `eval_set()` — `_runner/run.py` uses it only for the display and the store. **The two channels were never meant to carry the same value**, which is a correction to exec §4 and config §8.2, and reads had already been doing the right thing.

Measured, one flow worker against a 3.0s plain-`eval_set()` baseline: **4.36s → 4.14s** on an empty log directory, **4.79s → 4.21s** with 150 logs in it. The growing term is gone (it was ~2.4ms per log, so ~12s per worker at five thousand), and the residual ~1.1s is **entirely the requirements freeze** — two `uv` shell-outs that cannot be skipped from outside, only redirected. The original attribution spread that 1.1s across resolution, `flow.yaml`, and the scan; measurement says otherwise, which sharpens what is still worth asking Flow for.

**Two other things came with it.** `--no-store-read --no-store-write` makes exec §5.4's "workers run with `store=none`" true, having been unimplemented — which leaves the store inert until step 33 hands both halves to Steward, and that is the right way round: what workers were writing were unattested claims. And `--set execution_type=inproc` fixes a **live bug**: a spec declaring `execution_type: venv` sent `flow run` to `venv_launch`, so every worker built a virtualenv and ran the eval in a grandchild — the pid Steward recorded, its discovery entry, and every liveness check keyed on them all naming the wrong process. That is the same instruction as hawk's `--direct`, so it is now stated once in config §8 as a rule for every adapter rather than twice as a detail.

**Hawk got no code and one correction, which is the honest outcome.** It exposes no `--log-dir` to redirect and no flag to skip, so nothing here reaches it. But the "actively unsafe" framing was wrong in three ways: **uv takes an exclusive lock on the target environment** (verified — two concurrent installs, one logs `Waiting to acquire exclusive lock`, both succeed), so concurrent installs serialize; **capture has already installed** into Steward's interpreter before `launch` spawns anything, so worker installs are satisfied no-ops; and the **downgrade hazard is an N=1 problem** that `steward tasks` triggers, not a fan-out one. What is genuinely N× is the remote reads — secrets and provider env — and those produce *process state*, so redirection cannot help and the ask needs a second half: the frontend must be able to **report** what it resolved, for `DefinitionCommand.env` to carry.

So **the serialization workaround is declined rather than deferred**, and *Hawk fan-out is not blocked*: it works, at the cost of N× startup remote calls and N× redundant installs. Waste and a throttling risk, not corruption.

**Review found three consequences of the two overrides, and each was taken a different way.** The `inproc` override also drops what a spec declares in `dependencies` and `python_version`, because the venv is where Flow applies them — so Steward now **warns** when a YAML spec asks for one, and does not refuse: the environment usually satisfies the spec, since the author provisioned it, and the check cannot see a declaration that arrives through an `include:` or a Python spec anyway (config §8). The scratch `--log-dir` leaves Flow's global `last_log_dir` pointing at a disposable directory, which is **accepted**: correcting it means writing another tool's user-global file, and the only knob that would redirect it is `HOME`, which a worker needs (config §8.2, exec §13 q1, now a second small ask of Flow). And the same write was reaching the *real* home from the test suite, which is neither accepted nor warned about — an autouse fixture redirects `HOME` and `XDG_DATA_HOME` for every non-network test (testing.md §3).

### Step 8 — In-flight record and liveness ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `.steward/inflight.jsonl` and `resolve_inflight(record, workers_dir)` — what was spawned, resolved against what is running, into the `InFlight` that step 5's `reconcile` already took. `tests/worker/test_inflight.py`, 18 cases, 14s, **three** eval workers launched plus four `python -c` processes that cost nothing; twelve of the cases are a table over a stubbed scan.

**One measurement inverted the design.** The plan treated the process scan as a recovery route — the record answers, and the table rebuilds one that was lost. Measured on a table of 756 processes, `process_iter` costs 52ms and `environ()` over all 544 same-user ones costs 6ms: **~60ms**, affordable on every tend. So the scan became the liveness source and the record shrank to what a process cannot tell you — that a spawn was attempted, and which task, attempt, and command a running worker belongs to. The scan is then *exercised*, where a path taken only after a crash is not.

**Review caught the consequence, which is that a marker in the environment is inherited.** Every subprocess an eval starts — a sandbox's `docker`, a frontend's `uv` — carries the same `INSPECT_EVAL_SET_SELECTION`, so the marker is a subtree test rather than a process test. The first draft collapsed matches by selection path and kept whichever came last, which is a child: the worker's socket lost, its pid wrong for anything step 11 would signal, and a task held open indefinitely by an orphan that outlived it. Two rules fix it. The scan keeps only the **ancestor-most** match of each subtree, which is unambiguous while the worker lives, since its own parent is the tend that spawned it and carries no marker. And where the record knows a pid, **that pid decides** — a selection whose only surviving process is a leftover child reads as departed. So the path match did not replace the pid; it made the pid safe to use. Neither half answers alone: a recycled pid fails the path test, and a descendant fails the pid test. One degradation survives and is pinned by a test rather than left implicit — with the record lost there is no pid to check against, so an orphaned child of a dead worker reads as the worker.

Three decisions:

- **`Fleet.spawn` writes both records**, rather than the caller bracketing it. Intent-before-spawn is a safety property, and safety properties belong inside the mechanism they protect — the same argument spawn.py already makes for its two log directories. `Fleet.inflight` is required for the same reason: an argument that can be forgotten eventually is.
- **`RunningWorker` split into two types.** It could not express an `intent` with no `launched`, which has no pid, and inventing one would put a number in the record that never named a process — so `DepartedWorker.pid` is `int | None`. The other half is the interesting one: `RunningWorker.socket` is `None` until the worker binds one, which makes the pre-boundary window **a state a summary can report** rather than something inferred from two absences.
- **The JSONL mechanics moved to `_util/jsonl.py`**, shared with the journal, which wants the same three properties for the same reason: both are read after something went wrong. Durability is where they differ, so `append_event` takes `sync` — `True` for the one file nothing can rebuild, `False` for the one the next resolve reconstructs.

Two corrections to execution.md, both from §7.1 claims the scan invalidated. **Process start time is no longer a correctness mechanism** — it was there to defeat pid recycling, which the selection-path match now does better; it stays as provenance. And **`launched` cannot carry the control socket**: it is bound at the `eval_set()` boundary, long after the spawn returns, so it is discovered rather than recorded. §7.2 narrowed to match — discovery supplies the socket of a worker already known to be running, not the liveness answer.

**And a finding that matters more than the step: since step 7, every test had been running without a control surface.** The autouse home-directory fixture put the fake home under `tmp_path_factory`, and inspect binds its control socket at `<data dir>/inspect_ai/control/<pid>.sock` — which under `/private/var/folders/../pytest-of-user/pytest-93/popen-gw6/home0` exceeds the 104-byte `sun_path` limit. Inspect warns and runs on without one, so nothing failed; the socket assertion here is the first thing that asked. The fake home is now made under `/tmp` (testing.md §3). Step 9 is entirely control-channel client work and would have hit this head-on with no clue where to look.

### Step 9 — The control channel client ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `_worker/ctl.py` — reading the fleet and retuning a worker, with every outcome against a worker that has already gone as a value rather than an exception. `tests/worker/test_ctl.py`, 16 cases, 18s, one launch.

**The step got smaller because the client is `inspect ctl`.** The plan was an in-process `httpx` client over the AF_UNIX routes; the measurement that killed it is that **`inspect ctl task list` spans every live process in one call**, reading them concurrently and stamping each row with its pid. A fleet read is 1 × 1.3s, not N × 1.3s, which was the whole case for going in-process. What comes with the CLI is worth more than the round trip: a closed error-kind vocabulary, the busy/retry policy (the control server shares the eval's event loop, so **a timeout means alive-but-busy and a connection error means gone** — reads retry, mutations never do), and `--author` / `--reason` provenance. Steward runs it as `[sys.executable, "-m", "inspect_ai._cli.main", "ctl", …]`, the same in-this-interpreter pattern `definition_command` uses for the frontends. No `httpx`, no `anyio`, no async core, no hand-rolled classification.

Three decisions:

- **Primitives are `inspect ctl`'s; compositions are Steward's** — the doctrine this step exists to settle, now in exec §8.2. It also answers the question that prompted it: Steward's CLI stays small not because it is forbidden verbs but because most worker operations are single directives against single targets, which an agent runs itself against a CLI that already has an agent output contract. What Steward builds commands for is invalidate-and-resume, requeue, and a fleet-wide latch — operations with preconditions nobody should re-derive from a prompt.
- **Only two functions, because only two have callers.** `list_tasks(pids)` and `task_config(...)`. `pause` was in the scope line and is **not** here: exec §6.4 declines automatic model pause, workflow §3.1 puts `steward pause` on the scheduling side with no channel involved, and its one real consumer is step 25's composition — which is not obviously served by a single-target wrapper. Writing it now would be exactly the speculative surface the doctrine argues against.
- **Steward models the envelopes, because upstream does not.** Checked first: every producer returns `dict[str, Any]`, the routes are `-> Any`, and `_cli/ctl` holds no `BaseModel` or `TypedDict` either — the contract is held by upstream's tests, not by a type Steward could import. (The three `TypedDict`s in `_control/requeue.py` are the exception, and step 25 should reuse them.) So Steward validates the fields it depends on at the boundary, `extra="allow"`. That earned its place within the hour: `persisted` is **per-knob** (`{"max_samples": true}`), not the bool the read's `null` suggested, and the boundary is where that surfaced instead of a `KeyError` somewhere downstream.

**Two corrections to execution.md, and one to scheduling.md.** §8.2's *writes go through the supervisor* rested on the supervisor holding the accounting; `--author` / `--reason` put every applied change in the eval log, so it is in one place both parties write to, and the rule that survives is narrower — *Steward never undoes a latch it did not set*. §8.5 said `POST /config`; it is `PATCH` at two scopes, and `max_samples` is on the **task** one, which makes the fleet listing a precondition for retuning rather than an independent call (sched §3.2 now says so).

**And §8.5's open hazard is closed by measurement.** `time_limit`, `token_limit`, and `message_limit` are patchable *and* are inputs to `task_identifier`, so a patched value written back into `eval.config` would leave a finished task reading as an orphan. It does not happen: patching a `token_limit` on a live worker and recomputing the identifier from the log it went on to land yields the identifier the manifest scheduled. Pinned as a test, because it is a correlation property that would fail silently.

One thing worth carrying forward for the budget: **an `inspect ctl` invocation costs ~1.3s** — cheaper than a launch, dearer than anything else — which is why the live test folds polling and pid-filtering into one call, and why the gated fixture's sleep is now settable.

### Step 10 — The run claim ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `_workspace/claim.py` — `acquire`, `Claim`, `Held`, `read_claim` — plus `Workspace.claim` at `.steward/claim`. `tests/workspace/test_claim.py`, 19 cases, 7s serial, no evals; five `python -c` claim holders and seven bare sleepers that exist only to be live pids a payload can point at. Nothing calls it yet: `tend` is step 13 and `launch` step 16, and both rest on it.

**The mechanism is `fcntl.flock`, and the reason is the case that actually happens.** exec §5.7 specified a pid-keyed registry with a heartbeat, judged stale by age. Four semantics were verified on macOS rather than assumed, and the fourth is the one that reshaped the step: another process is blocked, a **second descriptor in the same process is also blocked**, a live holder is blocked, and a **killed holder's lock is acquirable immediately** — the kernel releases it. So a tend that crashed, was Ctrl-C'd, or was OOM-killed leaves *nothing to reap*: no timeout to sit through, no age to compute, no clock to be wrong about. The same-process case is not a curiosity either — it means the double-invoke a coding agent produces is caught by the same mechanism as the double-invoke a timer produces, and that eight of the twelve tests need no subprocess.

**Breaking a wedged holder is on by default, and that reversed the first plan.** A holder alive past the threshold is the only staleness `flock` leaves, and it cannot be taken without killing. Escalate-by-default was the first answer and it was wrong on its own terms: escalation is worth nothing at 2am, which is the hour the product exists for — the same argument §8.3 already makes when it refuses to let a launch-time warning stand in for an armed timer. What makes killing safe is step 8: workers are detached and outlive their supervisor, an `intent` with no `launched` resolves as departed and respawns, a torn journal line costs one line, and the process scan means a half-finished tend's workers are not spawned twice. A tend is built to be interrupted, so breaking one is the ordinary path rather than a hazard. `--no-break-claim` inverts it for someone attached who would rather examine the wedge. The cost is named rather than hidden: a *deterministic* wedge becomes a kill loop, journaled each round.

Three decisions:

- **`STALE_AFTER = 30 minutes`, and `stale_after` is a parameter.** Ten minutes was the first number, on the reasoning that a tend takes seconds — true locally, false for `observe_logs` over a few thousand logs in S3, and a threshold tuned for the local case would kill healthy tends on exactly the deployments that most need supervising. The threshold has to clear the slowest *honest* tend. Being a parameter is what a later `_steward.yaml` key will set, and it is also what lets the break tests run against a live process and a real clock instead of a backdated payload.
- **The payload is evidence, not authority** — the correction review forced, and the more important half of the step. Release truncates the file and a crash between locking and writing leaves it empty, so an unheld claim never reads as its last holder; the first draft treated that as sufficient and said so in exec §5.7. It is not. **Taking the lock and writing the payload are two operations**, so for the instant between them the file still names the previous holder, which is a live pid to signal once that pid has been reused. Three things are now re-established before anything is killed: a positive pid, this host, and a process that started *before* the claim's own instant — nothing can have recorded a claim before it existed, which is the general answer to recycling rather than a patch for one window. Success is still judged by re-taking the lock rather than by polling the pid: a killed holder nothing has reaped is still a pid, while its descriptors went at exit. And the check runs before **each** signal rather than once — two tends breaking one wedge is the ordinary race, and the loser waits out the whole grace period before it would escalate, by which time the holder is dead and the claim is the winner's. Re-reading turns that into an immediate stop instead of a `SIGKILL` aimed from stale notes.
- **`Claim.broke` carries what was killed, rather than journaling from inside.** `claim.py` has no business knowing what a journal is, and step 13 is where a tend writes its other events.

**Four corrections to execution.md, one each to workflow.md, testing.md, and roadmap.md.** §5.7 loses the registry, the heartbeat, and the long-lived supervisor — all three already rejected by §8.3 and surviving only there — and gains the lock, the break, and the reason the claim is keyed on the workspace rather than on `log_dir` (which two workspaces can share, and which is frequently S3). Its restart paragraph still pointed liveness at control discovery, which step 8 replaced with the process table. §10's clock paragraph stops calling itself "safe by accident": the crash case never reaches a clock now, and the wedge case has two asymmetric directions — backwards refuses, forwards over-breaks, and over-breaking costs one tend. §8.1's caveat pointed at a heartbeat note that no longer exists. testing.md's fault table splits *stale the claim* into the crash and the wedge, which are now different mechanisms.

**Review also found that a corrupt pid field reaches `os.kill` as a process *group*.** `{"pid": 0}` signals the caller's own group and a negative pid signals another; the first draft's integer check accepted both. Demonstrated rather than reasoned about — reverting the guard and running the suite killed the test runner and the shell around it. Non-positive pids are now dropped at the payload boundary, which is the right place: a field that cannot name a process should never become one.

**And one hazard the mechanism cannot defend against, found while writing the docs.** `flock` attaches to the inode, so unlinking `.steward/claim` while a claim is held leaves the holder holding it and lets the next tend create a fresh file, lock that, and run concurrently — and nothing on the new file's side can detect it, because the old inode is unreachable by name. workflow.md advertises `.steward/` as disposable, so this is a real thing a person can do. It joins the starting-worker case under the same rule: delete it when nothing is running.

### Step 11 — Fault-injection harness 🔧 ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `tests/evalset/fixtures/faulty_evalset.py` (a definition that fails wherever it is told to), `tests/_fault.py` (the shared waiting and arming), and `tests/worker/test_faults.py`. Five tests, 23s serial, three launches and three captures. testing §4's table now names, per row, the module that falsifies it — or the step that will.

**The inventory was the surprise: ten of the sixteen faults already passed.** Not one had been written as fault injection; each arrived as the natural test of the step that built its subject. That is the strategy working rather than an accident, and it changed what the step was. It is not a suite — it is three pieces of shared machinery, one real bug, and the column that makes *the faults are expressible* checkable rather than asserted.

**The bug is that deleting `.steward/` mid-run was not safe, and both docs said it was.** testing §4 and workflow §5.1 drew the line at *while a worker is starting*; the line was in the wrong place. The directory holds the in-flight record **and** every selection document, and the scan read a worker's *identity* from the document — so a deletion left a live worker the next tend could see but not name. Its task then read as a `started` log with nothing running it, which reconciles to a respawn **with `resume` pointing at the log the first worker is still writing**, and upstream recovers that log from a live buffer database. Not "a duplicate that reads as an ordinary retry": two processes over one task's log state.

**The fix is step 8's inversion finished.** The scan was half process-table — liveness from `environ()`, identity from a file. `_worker_env` now stamps `STEWARD_WORKER` and `STEWARD_TASK` beside the marker, `scan_processes` reads them in the sweep it was already doing, and `_selected_identifier` is deleted rather than kept as a fallback. Both faults become safe in different ways, which is why they are pinned separately: mid-eval the worker is seen running and nothing is scheduled over it; mid-startup it has not read its document yet, so the deletion kills it — and a dead worker *should* be respawned, once, from nothing. Both were confirmed to fail against the pre-fix scan before being asserted.

Three decisions:

- **`reached` and `go`, not a delay.** The fixture writes `<point>.reached` on arrival and `hang` waits for `<point>.go`, so testing §4's *inject at decision points, never at wall-clock times* is the only thing it can do. `slow` was dropped from the taxonomy for the same reason — a delay is a race and a gate is a state. That took `STEWARD_TEST_SLEEP=120` out of `test_ctl.py` and a 5-second sample out of `test_inflight.py`, which were the last wall-clock waits in the worker suite. `run:hang` waits on the event loop rather than blocking it, because the control server shares that loop and an unreachable held worker is the opposite of the point.
- **Three points, and `post` earns its place.** `pre`, `run`, `post` are the whole taxonomy of a worker's lifetime, and the third is the only way to observe a landed log whose process has not exited — fault row 1, which had only its benign half.
- **testing §7 q1 closed: a fixture.** The safety argument (keeping a process-killing capability out of the package) was the weaker one. What settled it is that nothing in `src/` would import any of it — the two pieces that looked most like a tool turned out to be an eval definition and a `chmod`, and the `chmod` was cut for having no caller yet.

**Review found the harness's own hazard, which is that a held worker is a leak.** A worker is detached so a run outlives its tend, and a test that fails or times out while holding one leaves it spinning on a marker nothing will ever write — pytest exiting does not touch it. Two mechanisms, because one failure mode each. An autouse fixture sweeps the test's workspace at teardown with the production scan, which is the right tool because a leak is by definition what no `finally` caught; and a hold watches for its spawner disappearing and exits, covering the case teardown cannot — pytest killed, or Ctrl-C. The second is a state rather than a timeout: the ppid captured at startup against the current one, so a subreaper adopting the orphan reads the same as init doing it. Both were verified against a deliberately leaking test rather than reasoned about.

`until` had drifted into three copies, which is what moved it to `tests/_fault.py`; `gated_evalset.py` is gone, absorbed. `slow_evalset.py` and `raises_early.py` stay — they are *capture* fixtures, a different subject with a different arming condition, and the new one is armed only in worker mode so that reading a manifest stays free.

It landed here rather than at the end because this is the first point where every recovery claim has machinery behind it, and because the harness then grows with each later step instead of being retrofitted onto all of them at once. The inventory is the evidence that it was late rather than early — but it found a bug that had been in the design docs since they were written, so not too late.

### Step 12 — `_steward.yaml` ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `_workspace/directives.py` — `Directives`, `read_directives`, `resolve_pool`, `REFUSED` — plus `Workspace.directives` at `_steward.yaml`, and the template `init` writes in place of `policy.md`. `tests/workspace/test_directives.py`, 46 cases, 3.7s, no evals and no processes.

**Moved ahead of the tend loop**, from where it sat after `launch`. The step-13 design pass produced two settings with no home — the non-convergence threshold and the digest cadence — on top of the tend interval §5.9 had already stranded. Building tend first would have meant inventing a stub home and replacing it, which is the cost this reordering avoids.

- **One file, not two, and the merge is the deliverable.** `policy.md` becomes the prose half of `_steward.yaml`, under YAML front matter Steward acts on alone. The reason is not tidiness: the line between *executable* and *interpreted* is a fact about Steward's current capability rather than about the author's intent, and it moves as Steward improves — so two files would make the reader's model track the implementation. It also closes a hole with no other answer, since a pre-authorised rule written only as prose fires only when an agent is in session, which is exactly when it is not needed. workflow §5.10.
- **Exactly one key, `max_workers`.** The rule turned out to be sharper than *does Steward need this*. `max_samples` is needed, is written into every selection document, and is still **refused by name**, because `eval_set(max_samples=...)` can say it and a workspace constant would silently outrank an author who did. `max_workers` passes in the other direction: the fan-out into processes is Steward's invention and no `eval_set()` argument reaches it. The test is whether the *definition* could express it.
- **`headline_metric` was proposed and rejected on the same ground.** One workspace-wide answer to a question each task answers differently. It belongs in the log header where every reader agrees — roadmap §5, **upstream item 14**, which has since landed — with an interim convention in step 14, since retired for `EvalResults.headline`.
- **The body is never parsed.** The agent opens the file itself, so no prose, however malformed, can break a tend.
- **Strict validation, and the first attempt got this wrong.** Typing the keys looked like the answer to YAML coercion — the hazard §5.3 could dismiss for the journal and cannot here, since a human writes this file. It is not: a coercive validator composes with YAML's coercion instead of cancelling it, and `max_workers: yes` went `True` → `1`, silently throttling a fleet to one worker. Caught in review. `strict=True` refuses it, and the error names the value that arrived, because seeing `not True` is the only way an author learns what YAML did to what they typed.
- **Strict now, degrading later.** A malformed file refuses the command. Falling back to the last known good is the better behaviour for a file someone may edit at 10pm with a fleet up, and it needs the per-tend `observation` record to read the last good values from — so it lands with step 13, not here.
- **§5.3 is distinguished rather than contradicted.** It rejected markdown-with-front-matter for `journal.jsonl` because block delimiters fail *globally* across thousands of appended records. One human-authored block read at startup has a single fence to get wrong, and an unterminated one is reported rather than absorbing the file.

**Retrospective — the format outlived its argument, and the argument did not.** The last three bullets above describe a markdown file with a YAML fence, which is what shipped here and what the file stopped being in the configuration pass after step 21. Every claim in them was true and two of them are why the fence went: *the body is never parsed* meant the delimiter was separating Steward's region from a region Steward only had to not read, and *strict validation* is the part that carries forward unchanged. What broke the arrangement was the three-spelling rule — once every setting also had to be sayable from the environment and the command line, a format where one half was YAML and the other was not made the mapping awkward for no gain. `policies` is now a key like any other. The one property genuinely lost is that malformed prose can no longer be harmless; the degrade path step 13 added, one bullet below, is what makes that survivable.

### Step 13 — The tend core ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `_tend/` — `tend`, `status`, `TendResult`, `Refused`, `status_markdown` — with `steward tend` and `steward status` over it; `_evalset/archive.py`; `write_manifest`/`read_manifest`/`definition_hash` and `Workspace.manifest`, so desired state is finally *stored* somewhere; `_workspace/log.py`; and in `reconcile`, `ArchiveLog`, the stall guard, `Pool.stall_after`, `Summary.stalled`/`archiving`, and `InFlight.spent`. Tests: `test_tend.py` (19, no evals), `test_tend_cli.py` (11), `test_tend_live.py` (1 test, **3 launches**), plus new `test_manifest.py`/`test_log.py` and additions to `test_reconcile.py` and `test_directives.py`.

**Done when** a repeated tend is a no-op and a tend interrupted at any point is recovered by the following one — both hold, the second by deleting the journal and `status.md` between two turns and getting the same answer.

- **The convergence guard is a repair, not a feature.** An action is safe unattended when it is non-destructive, derived from a state rather than a timeout, idempotent, and **convergent** — and `SpawnWorker` failed the fourth: nothing stopped a task being respawned forever. The signal is progress rather than attempt count, since a task on attempt four with 490 of 500 samples done is converging and one repeating the same twelve failures is not.
- **It needed a second half nobody had specified.** Progress-across-`superseded()` reads the log directory, and the failure roadmap §1 calls the probable one for a large sweep — a definition that will not import, an OOM at startup — **lands no log at all**, so every turn sees the identical `missing` and respawns forever, invisibly. The record is the only witness, so `resolve_inflight` now counts spent attempts per identifier and a task with no logs stalls on that count instead.
- **Which exposed a real defect in step 5.** `SpawnWorker.attempt` was `len(superseded) + …`, i.e. derived from logs alone — so a worker that lands no log is attempt 1 forever, and since the attempt *names the worker*, every respawn overwrote the previous in-flight entry. The record could not count what it was being asked to count. `attempt` is now `max(logs, spent) + 1`; the larger rather than the sum, because an attempt that landed a log is in both.
- **And a second one, in review: the attempt number is an estimate, and the stem cannot be.** Both inputs to that `max` are disposable, so two landed logs plus a deleted `inflight.jsonl` number the next attempt 3 when 3 has already been used. `Fleet.spawn` now advances past whatever stems the workers directory already holds and records the number it actually used — uniqueness enforced by the namer, which is the one party that can see what names are taken.
- **And a third, which was the same mistake in the opposite direction.** `An invalidation clears it` was implemented as `return False` — a permanent exemption, not a reset. A retry that dies before landing a replacement leaves the invalidated log current, so the branch had no ceiling at all: an import error under an invalidated log respawns every ten minutes for as long as the run lasts. The clause now forgives what happened *before the human acted* and counts what happened after. When they acted is the log's **mtime** — invalidating rewrites the log, and nothing else touches a finished one — which is the only record of it there is; resetting at the log's `created` instead would have made invalidation useless as the unstick it exists to be, since a crash loop's crashes all postdate the log they follow. `LogAttempt.mtime` was already carried and had **no reader**, so nothing had ever exercised its units: `EvalLogInfo` normalizes to milliseconds, and reading it as seconds lands in the year 58614. A test now pins the unit.
- **Merging the two halves of the guard, also in review.** Consulting the record only when a task had *no* logs left the mixed case open: one partial log and then twenty crashes at import reads as one attempt that made progress, and respawns forever. So `InFlight.spent` carries each finished attempt's **start time** rather than a count, and the spent attempts that began after the newest log did are folded into the same fruitless run. The times are load-bearing — a count cannot tell a task recovering from crashes apart from one that got somewhere and then started crashing.
- **Archiving orphans is mechanical, because the gate is somewhere else.** Step 5's notes deferred this to 16/26 while workflow §2.3 says *anything that archives — escalate, always*. Both are satisfied by putting the gate at **commit** time (launch, step 16, `--accept-archive`): a one-character arg edit and a deliberate removal read identically, and only at the moment the manifest is committed is a human present and the delta showable. Once desired state says a task is not in the eval set, converging toward it is bookkeeping. Orphans only — superseded attempts of live tasks wait for signoff. A running orphan is reported and its worker left alone. And it is never a delete: the sibling `logs-archive/`, journaled with its reason and its destination.
- **`paused` suppresses archiving too, and still reaps.** A paused run makes no changes to itself and a move is a change; reaping only records what already happened, which stays true either way.
- **Drift is report-only.** One hash of the definition file against `manifest.source.content_hash` each turn, one line in the summary and `status.md`. It costs one `sha256` and closes the gap where a definition edited weeks ago is silently not what is running. Capture and drift agree *by construction* — `read_eval_set` calls the same `definition_hash` — so the only test that can fail is the live one, where a real capture is followed by a real turn.
- **`_steward.yaml` degradation landed as planned, and needed `steward.log` to land with it.** Strict parsing stays; the *caller* degrades, recovering `max_workers`/`max_samples`/`stall_after` from the most recent `observation` and saying so. With no prior observation there is nothing to fall back to and the turn refuses, since defaults would silently discard what the operator wrote. Where the *reason* goes was the open question, and the answer was neither the journal (it is not a fact about the eval set) nor step 22: a **minimal** `steward.log` writer, ~15 lines, that never raises. Step 22 still owns rotation, bounding, and the sync.
- **`_tend/` is its own package, not `_schedule/tend.py`.** The plan said the latter; it is an import cycle — `_schedule` → `_workspace` → `directives` → `_schedule` — that only breaks when `_workspace` is imported first. The turn is the composition of all four subsystems, so it sits above them rather than inside one.
- **Pause moved to step 15**, where the timer creates the need for a brake. `reconcile` has taken `paused` since step 5; nothing persists or sets it yet.

`status.md` is here rather than with the sync because writing it is part of what a turn *is* (workflow §3), and because M2 wants a human-readable snapshot even though nothing syncs yet. It is deliberately thin — step 14 owns the real surface.

### Step 13a — The header cache and the progress table ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `_evalset/cache.py` (`AttemptCache`, `read_attempt_cache`, `write_attempt_cache`, `Workspace.observed`); `_worker/live.py` (`read_fleet` over the worker's own socket); `_tend/progress.py` and `_tend/table.py`; `LogAttempt.mtime`/`headline`/`limits` and `ObservedLogs.locations`. Tests: `test_cache.py` (17), `test_live.py` (14), `test_progress.py` (14) — **no launches**, the live ones against a real AF_UNIX server in a thread.

**Not a planned step.** It came out of one question — *is `status` cheap enough to run constantly, and does it say enough to be worth running?* — asked because the operator's previous system slowed down late in a campaign, which is the point at which a status command is most wanted. Both halves turned out to be the same step 14 dependency, so they landed together, ahead of it.

**The cost was the shape it was, not the amount.** A turn already avoided the expensive path — a full read of a 500-sample log is 426ms and Steward reads headers, which are O(1) in samples at ~1.4ms. But it read *every* header *every* turn, so the per-turn cost grew with the campaign while the tend interval did not. A finished log is immutable and the listing already carries `mtime` and `size` for 0.011ms with no file opens, so the mutation signal is free and the re-read is the waste. Measured at 800 settled logs: **0.862s cold, 0.058s warm** — and one new log the next turn is still 0.058s, which is the property that matters, since it says the cost tracks *change* rather than *size*.

**The table needed a second source, and it is not the log.** Sample counts, the headline metric, and the declared limits are in the header; how many samples are running, how much of the limit the leading one has spent, and how hard the model pool is working exist only in the process. Reading those through `inspect ctl` was measured at ~1.6s per invocation and the connection pool resolves one task at a time — nineteen seconds for a fleet of ten, which is not a slow table but a table nobody runs. Over the workers' own sockets it is ~6ms each, concurrently. So **reads moved to `live.py` and every mutation stayed in `ctl.py`**, with the retry policy deliberately inverted: a mutation waits out a busy event loop because it has to land, and a table reports the row as busy and renders it from the log. Nothing live is read at all when nothing is running, which is the common late-campaign shape.

**Both halves are disposable by construction.** The cache is derived data — absent, unreadable, or from another version all mean *empty*, and a write that fails is a turn that was still correct. A worker that does not answer costs its own row's live columns. Neither can fail a tend, which is what let them go in below the turn rather than beside it.

This takes the **body table** and the **interim headline convention** out of step 14, which keeps the item list, the diff, and the verdict.

**Split from step 13 because the turn and its rendering are separately testable**, and because 13 is what steps 15–16 depend on.

**One list, not two, because responsibility is assigned rather than intrinsic.** The earlier draft had an attention list for the human and a work list for the agent, which hard-codes a routing decision that is not Steward's to make: one workspace's `_steward.yaml` may let the agent rule on a class the next one reserves for a person. So there is one list with an `owner`, and the projections are a filter over it. Owner is a function of **(kind, state, policy)** rather than of kind alone — an anomaly is the agent's while it needs grouping and the human's once there is a proposal to answer — and it is **recomputed every turn**, so editing the rules re-routes items that already exist. One kind is fixed by design and policy may not widen it: a parked worker is always the human's, because nobody else may answer ([agent.md](agent.md), *What the agent may do without asking*).

**The item points at its subject; it does not contain it.** An anomaly carries nine fields of its own ([workflow.md](workflow.md), *Anomalies are structured state*), and an envelope that absorbs them is one every later step rewrites. So an item is `id`, `kind`, `owner`, `level`, `subject`, and `summary`, plus the command that resolves it where there is one — and `subject` is a task identifier, a pid, or a class key.

**Items stay a projection, and *remains until resolved* comes from the subject rather than from the list.** There are two exit paths and only one of them needs memory. A condition that ends — a park answered, drift applied, a stall that starts progressing — stops being observed, and durability here would mean garbage-collecting items whose condition is already over. Something needing a *decision* persists because the subject is still open in the fold, not because the list remembered it. What that leaves uncovered is the third case, and it is the one this step adds an event for: a real condition nothing will clear mechanically, which somebody has already looked at and accepted.

**The id is the re-notification policy.** An acknowledgment is recorded against an item id, so what re-arms an item is exactly what changes its id — which makes the choice per kind a design decision rather than a naming one:

| kind | id | says it again when |
|---|---|---|
| `drift` | `drift:<content_hash>` | the definition changes again — and clears at relaunch for free |
| `stalled` | `stalled:<identifier>:<attempts>` | another attempt is made and also fails |
| `degraded` | `degraded:<mtime>` | the file is edited and still will not parse |
| `orphan_running` | `orphan:<identifier>` | never; the condition ends when the worker exits |
| `unreadable` | `unreadable:<name>` | per file, so one bad file is not a permanent count |
| `parked` (20) | `parked:<pid>:<request>` | the worker asks something else |
| `anomaly` (23) | `anomaly:<Name>:<digest8(class)>:g<n>[:x<bucket>]` | a ruling closes the window ([workflow.md](workflow.md), *The window closes when someone rules*), so recurrence is generation `n+1` — a new id over the old rulings as precedent; the bucket is the count's order of magnitude, so a population crossing 10, 100, 1000 re-arms the edge without every instance being news |

`action_failed` is deliberately absent. It is a single-turn fact that either recurs or does not, and making it acknowledgeable would give it a lifecycle it has not earned.

**A disposed item stops being reported, and its record leaves in the other direction.** Once acknowledged it is gone from the summary, from `status.md` as the next turn rewrites it, and from the channel, and it stops counting toward the verdict — a run whose last open item was accepted reads ✅, which is what disposal means. The trail is the journal event, plus the rule that keeps *the journal has it* from meaning *nobody ever reads it*: **an acknowledgment accepting something the data carries flows to `anomalies.md`** ([workflow.md](workflow.md), *The caveats that reached the final data*), and from there into signoff. One with no effect on the data simply ends.

**`acknowledged` disposes only of items whose subject has no lifecycle of its own**, and the boundary needs stating before step 23 arrives to find two ways of closing the same thing. Drift, a degraded read, a running orphan, an unreadable file: no class, no evidence, no proposal, nothing to rule *on* — someone looked and accepted it, and that is the whole of the act. An anomaly already has a richer path and keeps it, closing through `ruling` — whose accepting dispositions settle the window directly — and, for a pending re-run, through `resolution`. Both routes end the same way — the item stops being projected — which is what lets the list have one disposal rule while the subjects have two. It is also not `steward resolve` (exec §8.2), which acts on *samples*; `ack` disposes of a **line in a list** and touches nothing in the run.

**`steward ack` ships here rather than with adjudication, and the agent is its main caller.** An event nothing can write is inert, and inert means it can only be exercised against hand-written journals rather than through the surface a person actually uses. It cannot be a flag on `status`, which stays read-only (exec §8.4). A **reason is required** and the actor is recorded — the discipline `inspect ctl` already imposes on every applied change, and what makes workflow §17's promise (*who decided, and why*) true of this act too. `by` genuinely varies: an agent disposing of a transient it investigated is the agent's own ack, not a human's relayed through it. The guardrail that creates belongs in [agent.md](agent.md) §6 rather than in code — **acking is ask-first**, because an agent that can silently clear its own attention list has removed the gate on its own autonomy.

**Three renderings over one model, and 76 characters is the binding one.** Terminal, markdown, and Slack. Slack has no tables, so one is preformatted inside a code block at a hard 76 columns — narrower than the terminal, and narrower than a full display key needs. Converting markdown to Slack is the wrong direction for the hard part: the fixed-width renderer 13a already built **is** the Slack table at `width=76`, and what remains to convert is prose, where it is mechanical. A post is the verdict line (which is also its title), then the items, then the table. There is no row budget — a sweep is five to ten tasks, and rationing rows for a shape that does not occur is the wrong instinct.

**The display key expands from the task name rather than shrinking toward it.** `compute_display_keys` already renders only the args that *differ* when two tasks collide; this applies the same rule one level up. A row shows its task name, and gains `@model`, then `[solver]`, then the manifest's full key, only where a name collides — and per colliding group, so one row can read `alpha` while two others read `beta@gpt-5` and `beta@claude`. That is where the width comes from: `sec_bench_pro[default]@openai/gpt-5` is thirty-five characters and `sec_bench_pro` is thirteen, which is the difference between a row that fits at 76 with every column and one that does not. It works from the structured fields already on the observation rather than by re-parsing a key Steward built, because an orphan's key is a bare log task name with no shape to parse. A universal model is stated once beside the totals; a universal `[default]` is not, being the absence of information rather than a fact about the run.

**The verdict is a level, not an event.** ✅ nothing needs you · ⚠️ something does, work continues · 🛑 nothing progresses until a person answers · ⏸ paused. Computed run-level rather than item-level, as `max()` over the items' own levels: one parked worker among twenty running is ⚠️, because a rule that paints red whenever any single thing is blocked makes red meaningless. The consequence — a `stopped`-kind post can carry a ⚠️ verdict — is deliberate, since the kind says *why you are hearing from me* and the glyph says *where the run stands*.

**Nothing is sent here.** Step 24 owns the channel and the kinds; this step owns the item, the diff, and the acknowledgment that stops the diff repeating itself. All three are things `status` needs whether or not anything is ever sent.

**Doc corrections this step owes.** agent.md §6 gains acking as ask-first, and §5's *render what it printed* is reconciled with there being three renderings rather than one. workflow §14 gains the path by which an acknowledgment reaches `anomalies.md`, and §5.6's journal vocabulary gains `acknowledged` — the one event kind an item list was not supposed to need.

### Step 15 — The timer ✅ **done**

**Delivered** `_timer/` — three backends behind one `Scheduler` protocol (launchd, systemd `--user`, cron), `detect` choosing between them per entry and failing when none fits, `arm`/`disarm`/`installed` recording the choice in the journal, and the `.env` diff that gates arming. Plus `steward timer arm|disarm|status`, `steward pause`/`steward resume` as `paused`/`resumed` journal events wired into `reconcile(paused=)`, the `unsupervised` and `timer_drift` items, `tend_interval` in `_steward.yaml`, `parse_duration`, `python -m inspect_steward`, `init_dotenv()` at CLI startup, and `_util/process.py`. Tests: `tests/timer/` (112), `test_duration.py` (20), `test_process.py` (8), additions to `test_items.py`, `test_tend.py`, `test_journal.py`, and `test_directives.py` — 179 tests and **no launches at all**.

**Done when** — both held: a launchd agent tended a real workspace on a one-minute interval with nothing attached to it, and a paused run spawned nothing while still reaping and reporting.

**Pause is here rather than at 13 because the timer is what creates the need for a brake.** Before an armed timer, not tending *is* pausing — the operator simply stops typing `tend`, and a flag would be a second way to express what already works. After it, a fleet reconverges every ten minutes whether or not anyone wants it to, and the only alternative to a flag is disarming the timer, which also stops the reaping and the reporting that make a paused run legible.

**What the implementation corrected.**

- **Pause is a journal event, not a file.** The plan said "persisting the flag" and the obvious home was `.steward/`, which is documented as safe to delete — so clearing a cache would silently *resume* an expensive run. Between the two directions this can fail in, a pause that outlives a wiped cache is recoverable and a resume nobody asked for is not.
- **Cron is refused for an interval it cannot express, rather than rounded to one it can.** `*/7 * * * *` means minutes 0, 7, … 56 — a step that restarts each hour, leaving a four-minute gap and then an eleven-minute one. Detection is therefore per *entry* rather than per machine: a box where cron is the only scheduler arms at ten minutes and is refused at seven.
- **Nothing on the tend path probes a scheduler.** Asking `launchctl` every ten minutes would undo step 13a, so arming records what it installed and every later turn compares that record against how long it has actually been since a tend. That is the better signal anyway — it catches a crontab somebody edited by hand, which no probe of Steward's own entry would.
- **`unsupervised` fires on a broken expectation, never on the absence of one.** A workspace nobody ever armed is being driven by hand and has not lost anything; telling that operator every ten minutes is how an attention list stops being read. Same rule one level down for `timer_drift`, which compares against the interval `_steward.yaml` *expressed* rather than one that resolved — found by arming a real timer with `--interval 1m` and being told, a second later, that the workspace wanted something else.
- **The environment check is a diff, not a requirement.** Steward cannot know which provider a definition uses, so it compares the arming shell against the workspace `.env` and names what would vanish. Only *names* are read out of the file. `GITIGNORE_ENTRIES` and the `init` template both gained `.env`, since suggesting people write one should not also be a way to commit their keys.
- **Two intermittent failures were diagnosed rather than retried, and only one of them was Steward's.** A second process-table sweep, added here and since removed, turned a latent bug in `scan_processes` into a frequent one: macOS surfaces a mid-read exit from `proc_environ` as a bare **`SystemError`**, which is not a `psutil.Error`, so a sweep guarded on psutil's own hierarchy fails under load. The sweep now lives in `_util/process.py`, which owns the guard once — and it stayed there after the second caller went away, because the exception tuple is the part a reader needs stated rather than re-derived. The second failure was in step 13a's fake control server and is recorded in §10, because the lesson is about the testing category rather than about this step.

**What review corrected afterwards, and the one thing it deleted.** Six findings, five of them local: a `launchd` disarm that returned quietly when `bootout` failed now re-probes and raises if the label is still loaded; `Cron.usable` checks for the binary before shelling out, since `run_command` *raises* on a missing one and would otherwise escape detection as an error on the machine most needing to be told; `dotenv_names` requires a value, because the two halves of the credential diff have to agree that `KEY=` carries nothing; the staleness half of `unsupervised` is gated on the turn not having executed, so a recovery tend does not announce the silence it just ended; and `timer.log` moved out of `.steward/` to the workspace root — a scheduler is handed one absolute output path at arming and never asked again, so a directory documented as safe to delete is the one place it cannot go.

**The sixth finding removed the fourth backend.** The ticker — a detached process of Steward's own, always usable — snapshotted the arming environment so that a scheduled tend had credentials at all. That is a second credential model: it works, it survives a `.env` rotation by ignoring it, and every later step that touches credentials (scout, first of all) would have had to reason about both. Deleting it left one model — a scheduler hands the tend a near-empty environment, `init_dotenv()` makes `.env` authoritative, and everything the tend launches inherits `os.environ` — and restored [execution.md](execution.md)'s *if none can be armed, the launch fails*, which an always-usable backend had made unreachable. What it cost is a machine with no cron, which is now told what it lacks instead of being handed a timer that dies with its terminal. That is the better failure, and it is the one that section was arguing for anyway.

**A second review round, and its through-line is that a failure has a direction.** Six more findings, none of them about whether the code works and all of them about which way it breaks when it does not.

- **An unreadable journal is an unanswered question, not an empty history.** `recorded` swallowed `OSError` and returned `None`, which reads as *nothing is armed* — so `steward timer disarm` would print exactly that while the timer it could not see went on tending, and nothing would be able to find it again. It raises now. The rationalisation in the old docstring ("the caller is about to write to it and will fail there") was true of `arm` and false of the two readers that matter.
- **An installed timer that nothing recorded is one nothing can remove.** The `armed` event is the only record of which backend holds the entry, and it was appended *after* arming — so a journal that could not be written left the machine tending every interval with no exit short of editing a crontab by hand. Arming now undoes itself if the event does not land. Of the two orderings this could take, the one that fails toward *recorded but absent* is recoverable — `timer status` says so, the item fires, `arm` is idempotent — and the one that fails toward *present but unrecorded* is not.
- **`.env` is read by python-dotenv now, not by a split on `=`.** `KEY=""`, `KEY=''`, `KEY=${UNSET}`, and a bare `KEY` are four more ways to write something that looks like an assignment and arrives as nothing, and the hand-rolled parser called them defined. Both halves of the diff have to agree about empty, and the cheapest way to guarantee that is to resolve values with the library that will resolve them again at 02:00. `${AMBIENT}` remains invisible and is documented as such: nothing here can know what environment cron will supply.
- **Arming closes the `.gitignore` gap rather than reporting it.** `init` gained the `.env` entry; workspaces created before it did not, and nothing re-runs `init` — so the command that tells somebody to put their API keys in `.env` is the one that has to make git ignore it. `ensure_gitignore` came out of `create.py` for the second caller. What it cannot fix is a file already tracked, which an ignore rule does not untrack.
- **cron reads the command field before any shell does, and `%` there is a newline.** Everything after the first unescaped one is cut from the command and fed to it on stdin, and `shlex.quote` cannot prevent it because cron gets there first. A `%` in a workspace path is unusual and entirely legal, and what it produced was a timer that silently ran half a command.
- **The staleness clock starts when the timer does.** The gap was measured from the last `observation` alone, so re-arming after a long silence reported the new timer as having been quiet for hours — making the item's own `action` look as though it had not worked. It now runs from the later of the arming and the last turn, which as a side effect closes the opposite hole: a run armed and never tended had no `observation` to measure from and so reported nothing at all.

### Step 16 — `steward launch` ✅

**Delivers** the entry point: a definition becomes a run.

- **Scope.** Capture; the five-row delta and the archive gate on it; commit the manifest as desired state; restore what the archive already holds; stop workers the commit orphaned; arm a timer or fail; record the launch in the journal; the first turn. Plus the initial concurrency allocation and the early refusal for a definition that declares a scanner.
- **Refs.** workflow §7, §2.3, §2.2; exec §8.3, §12 items 4/8/9; config §4; sched §2.2, §3.1–3.3.
- **Done when** a launch on a fresh workspace and a re-launch over a partial log directory both do the right thing.

**Last in the group, because launch is a composition rather than a component.** In the convergence model it is *capture, commit desired state, arm the timer, tend* — so it needs the tend core and the timer both, and building it earlier would mean stubbing the thing it is mostly made of. Tend is testable ahead of it against a hand-committed manifest and step 4's fixtures.

**Delivered** `_launch/` (`delta.py` pure, `launch.py` the composition), `_worker/stop.py`, `_cli/launch.py`, the `launched` journal event with `read_launched`, `restore_log` as `archive_log`'s inverse, `tend(claim=)`, and `ctl.cancel_task`. Upstream: the version-3 `EvalSetSelectionOverrides` container on a branch of inspect_ai, which discharged exec §12 items 4, 8, and 9 and half of 10. `tests/launch/`, 27 cases, 9s, **two launches** — one real capture and one real worker, both in `test_stop_live.py`; the rest fake the capture at the same seam the timer tests fake the crontab at. `tests/schedule/test_tend_live.py` was rewritten to go through `launch` for the same three launches it already spent.

**Four orderings, each with a wrong-looking alternative**, and they are the whole of what launch adds over its parts: the claim spans the capture (so a tend cannot interleave with the commit), the gate sits between capture and commit (so consent is taken while it can still be refused), arming precedes the first tend (so a launch whose turn fails is still supervised), and the restore follows the commit (because what a restore is *for* is the identifiers the new manifest asks for). All four are stated in the module docstring rather than left to a reader.

**The apparent contradiction with step 13's retro dissolved rather than needing a decision.** That retro says the gate is *orphans only — superseded attempts of live tasks wait for signoff*, which read as forbidding workflow §2.3's fourth row. It does not: an args edit produces a **new** identifier, so the old one *is* an orphan, and both archive rows are orphans differing only in whether a task of the same name and model survived. What waits for signoff is multiple attempts of a still-live identifier, which is a different thing.

**`epochs` is not in `task_identifier`, and that is what makes `extend` a row.** Verified rather than assumed, alongside the same question about the dataset slice that item 8 turned on: raising epochs keeps a task's identity and lifts the bar its existing log is measured against, so the next tend resumes rather than restarting. A delta that treated it as remove-and-add would have proposed archiving a directory of good results for a one-character edit.

**Four review findings after the step was green, and the first two are the interesting ones.**

**A gate built as *are any rows archiving?* answers no to the most expensive edit there is.** Changing `log_dir` leaves every identifier untouched, so the delta has no rows, reads as purely additive, commits unasked — and the first tend then finds an empty directory, calls every task missing, and re-runs the whole sweep while the real results sit somewhere nothing looks any more. Nothing is archived and nothing is deleted, which is exactly why the row-shaped gate could not see it. Fixed by making `additive` a property of the delta rather than a filter over its table, with a `Relocation` beside the rows; the count is *wanted, in the old directory, and not in the new*, so copying logs across before relaunching costs nothing. **The general lesson is that a predicate defined over a report's rows will miss anything the report has no row for**, and the gate was the one place in the step where that mattered.

**`--no-timer` skipped the arming instead of ending the supervision.** On a first launch those are the same thing; on a re-launch the previous timer keeps firing, `read_armed` keeps reporting it, and the operator has been told the opposite of what is true while expensive work goes on being scheduled against an explicit instruction. exec §8.3 says `--no-timer` *launches unsupervised*, which is a claim about the state left behind rather than about a step omitted — so it disarms, and a disarm that fails fails the launch exactly as a failed arm does. Between the two directions, the timer nobody wants is the one that spends money.

**A relocation left the fleet running, which is the same bug one layer down.** Fixing the gate stopped a silent re-run but not the wasted work: a worker's log directory is written into its selection document when it spawns and cannot be changed, so every worker alive at the commit goes on producing logs for a directory nothing reads — and its task is not respawned while it lives, so the loss is invisible until it exits and the task starts again from nothing. So a relocation stops the whole fleet, not only the workers named by archiving rows. Two consequences fell out. `Delta.stopping` unions two reasons and has to dedupe, since one worker can be both. And `compute_delta` now takes the fleet as a sequence rather than an identifier-to-stem mapping: a mapping collapses two workers on one identifier — which a `.steward/` deleted mid-run produces — leaving one of them writing into the abandoned directory, a hole in the fix itself.

**A safety default with no way out is a trap.** Omitting `-A` means *reuse the committed arguments*, which is what keeps a forgotten flag from capturing a different eval set. It also meant that once a launch had passed arguments, nothing asked for the definition's defaults again: no `-A` reuses and any `-A` sets. `--no-args` passes an empty mapping, and `{}` is now meaningfully different from `None` at the API — *no arguments* rather than *whatever you would default to*. Passing both is a usage error rather than a precedence rule nobody should have to remember.

Two smaller ones. `INSPECT_STEWARD_STORE` was never read, so `--store` was an override of nothing and a machine with a store configured recorded `null`; it now falls back, with an exported-but-empty variable read as unset and an empty value *typed* on the command line still a typo — the same distinction `_timer.env` draws about a credential. And the *nothing is written* guarantee was overbroad: `ensure_gitignore` necessarily precedes the credentials refusal, so a refused launch can touch `.gitignore`. That write is now logged where it happens rather than after the launch returns (a launch that raised was leaving it unaccounted for), and the guarantee is stated as *nothing about the run* — no manifest, no journal entry, no log moved, no worker stopped.

**One finding recorded rather than fixed, and it is owed to two later steps.** A cancelled worker's log lands with `status="error"` — inspect finalizes it with a `TerminateTaskError` — which `observe` reads as `IncompleteReason.ERROR` and `reconcile` therefore treats as a task worth trying again. Harmless for the only caller there is, since a launch stops only workers whose task has *left* the manifest and an orphan is archived rather than respawned. Not harmless for `steward stop` and for the smoke's wall-clock cap, which both stop workers whose tasks desired state still names: each will need something that says *do not start this again*, and neither a pause nor a per-identifier decision is `stop.py`'s to invent. Asserted in `test_stop_live.py` so that an upstream change shows up there rather than in a later verb's behaviour.

**Two path traps found by running the command from a subdirectory**, which is why the manual pass is in the plan. `read_eval_set` resolves what it is given against the *process* cwd — it hashes the file before any subprocess with a `cwd` of its own exists — so a relative path meant `steward launch` could not find its own workspace's definition from a child directory. And the path *recorded* has to be relative to the workspace root, because every later tend resolves it against the root. Three different paths, one argument: the manifest's `source.path` is rewritten after the capture rather than steered into it, which keeps `read_eval_set`'s contract simple and puts the reason where the one caller that needs them different can state it.

**`--store` parses and does nothing, and it is the one thing here that breaks a standing rule.** `_steward.yaml` ships one key per step precisely so that a knob which parses and does nothing is not a lie about what the workspace controls, and a flag is the same object. It is in because it was asked for; it validates only that the value is non-empty, records itself in the `launched` event, and the CLI help says when it starts mattering. Step 33 is where it becomes true.

**Retrospective — the knob was right to ship early and wrong in every detail of how.** It is `--log-store` now, with a `log_store` key and a `STEWARD_LOG_STORE` variable, and the three changes are three different mistakes. *Naming*: `store` collides with inspect's own `Store`, the per-sample scratch state a solver and a tool share, and a Steward run has both — `log_store` also stops the mechanism reading as Flow's, which §5.4 of [execution.md](execution.md) argues it never was. *Value shape*: `none` was a magic string in a field whose other values are locations, and it is a perfectly good directory name, so it is `false` now and refused by name if typed. *Surfaces*: the paragraph above defends a flag that overrides a variable, which was the whole arrangement — the location was environment-only on the grounds that a store is the machine's rather than the project's, true of a laptop's mirror and false of a team's S3 prefix. It is a key as well now, and the precedence orders the two instead of the design choosing between them. **Inert for seventeen steps and live at step 33**, which is the vindication the early ship was betting on: nothing about the surface had to change when the mechanism arrived under it, and the value shape it settled on — a location, `auto`, or `false` — turned out to be general enough to carry two implementations nobody had chosen yet.

**Retrospective — the two flags that shipped here were the ownership rule leaking.** `steward tend --max-tasks` and `--max-samples` said inspect's words on Steward's command line, in the same week `max_tasks` was refused from `_steward.yaml` by name. They are gone. What replaces them is not a third spelling but the recognition that **a Steward run is an `inspect eval-set` invocation with a longer lifetime**: inspect's own variables are honoured, `steward launch` carries inspect's own options generated from inspect's own model, and both write into inspect's own overrides document. Every identity-neutral `eval_set()` argument works that way, not the two that happened to be wanted — the bound is `task_identifier()`, enforced upstream by an exhaustiveness test ([execution.md](execution.md) §12 item 4).

### Step 17 — Fan-out width ✅

**Delivers** control over the shape of a run: how much of it is in flight, and how many processes that is divided into — so that a runtime whose per-process side effects dominate can be run whole instead of divided.

- **Scope.** Two keys, both unbounded by default: `max_tasks` bounds tasks in flight fleet-wide, `max_workers` bounds the processes they are poured into. The pour, dealing round-robin. The selection at width *k*, and the `max_tasks` override that makes a batch run concurrently. The in-flight record, the stem, and `STEWARD_TASK` at width *k*. Correlating a packed worker's control-channel rows back to identifiers. Stopping one task of a packed worker rather than the process.
- **Refs.** exec §2, §3, §4, §4.1, §7.3, §12 item 15; config §7; sched §1.1, §1.2, §2.1–2.4, §3.1.
- **Done when** the same manifest runs correctly at width 1, at width *k*, and at width *all* — and a worker at every width is found by the scan and reaped correctly.

**The motivation is that splitting is not always the cheaper side of the trade.** A process costs ~3s (step 1 measured it), and for Hawk it is `uv pip install` plus secrets resolution *per worker* — work step 7 attacked from the other side and could not reach, because Hawk exposes no knob to redirect or skip it. Five hundred small tasks is around half an hour of pure startup. Where a runtime's per-process side effects dominate like that, dividing the run is the expensive choice, and Steward needs a way not to make it. This is also the honest hedge against a frontend that turns out to behave badly under fan-out for a reason nobody predicted: run it whole, and supervise the one process.

**The selection document is written at every width, including *all*.** It is tempting to read *run it as the author intended* as *do not intercept it*, and that is the wrong shape — four things ride the selection document and each would have to be given up: the `INSPECT_EVAL_SET_SELECTION` path is the marker the process table is searched for (exec §7.3), the `log_dir` override is what puts logs where Steward is watching, the forced kwargs (`fail_on_error=False`, `task_retry_attempts=0`, `acp_server=True`) arrive through it, and skipping eval-set bookkeeping is what keeps `eval-set.json` and `logs.json` out of a directory Steward owns. Losing the ACP surface alone would reinstate the silent failure exec §7.4 exists to prevent. Width changes how many tasks one document names; it does not change whether there is one.

| `max_workers` | `max_tasks` | 500 pending | crash costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| — | — | 500 processes × 1 task | one task |
| — | 10 | 10 processes × 1 task, 490 queued | one task |
| 10 | — | 10 processes × 50 tasks | 50 tasks' partial progress |
| 1 | — | 1 process × 500 tasks | the run's partial progress |

**The width setting turned out to be two settings, and separating them is what made the step small.** A single *width* knob conflates how much runs with how it is packaged, and the two have nothing to do with each other: `max_samples` is per **task**, so concurrency is `tasks in flight × max_samples` whether those tasks are in one process or fifty. Split, each knob has one job and the defaults are both *unbounded* — which is also the correction to the shipped `max_workers: 10`, a limit nobody chose that throttled large sweeps and did nothing for small ones.

**The wire format needed one field, and the reason is a good one.** `EvalSetSelection.tasks` was already a list, so naming several tasks cost nothing. What did not work was leaving `max_tasks` alone: in selection mode `eval_set()` never reaches its own defaulting, so an unset value falls through to `eval()`'s rule — one task at a time for a single model — and a packed worker would have run its batch **sequentially**, having inherited a decision nobody made. It is now a v4 override that Steward writes unconditionally, exactly as it writes `log_dir` (exec §12 item 15). *All* enumerates the manifest rather than asking for a no-filter document: enumerating is conservative and is what a stale manifest makes visible rather than silently overrides.

**Everything else was Steward-side, and the pluralisation was the bulk of it.** `RunningWorker` / `DepartedWorker` / `ScannedWorker` / `LiveTarget` carry `identifiers`; the `intent` record carries a `tasks` array of `(identifier, key, attempt)` triples, which is where attempt belonged all along since it is a fact about a task; `SpawnWorker` splits into a process holding a tuple of `SpawnTask`. **Two things were made byte-identical at width 1 on purpose** — `worker_stem`, because `sha256` of a one-element join is `sha256` of the element, and `STEWARD_TASK`, which became newline-delimited rather than gaining a plural sibling. So the default width's on-disk and in-environment shapes did not move, and the process-table scan needed no branch.

Two smaller things fell out. **A packed worker's control-channel rows have to be named**, and `TaskRow.log_location` against the observation Steward already holds does it for free — at width 1 there is nothing to tell apart and the correlation is skipped entirely. And **stopping became per-task**: archiving one task of a packed worker must not signal the process, so `stop_workers` cancels the named tasks and escalates only where nothing wanted is left in the process.

**The cost to state rather than discover** is that a worker which dies partway through a batch leaves some of its tasks complete, some started, some untouched. That turned out **not** to be the eval-set bookkeeping exec §2 warns about, and the reason is that a task writes its own log whichever process it ran in: the completed ones have landed, the started ones resume, the untouched ones are missing, and `observe` already reads all three. Nothing was added for it. Packing still trades crash isolation for startup cost, monotonically, and the default stays one task per process — at one process a single failure costs the run's partial progress, which is the exposure Steward was built to remove. It is an escape hatch for runtimes that leave no better option, not a tuning knob to reach for.

**The unbounded defaults are the part to keep an eye on**, and were the user's call. Nothing now bounds a run's load unless somebody sets `max_tasks`, and sched §2.3's *memory is assumed adequate* rested on the old ceiling of ten: with Layer 2 pruning still absent upstream, a five-hundred-task sweep now constructs five hundred copies of the un-pruned manifest rather than ten. Both are one key to narrow. If it bites before pruning lands, the fix is a launch-time guard against a measured figure rather than a number in the defaults.

**Placed before worker startup at scale because it is the unblocked half of the same problem**, and because the escape hatch is worth having *before* M2 rather than after it discovers the need. `max_tasks` is the key `_steward.yaml` gains at this step and `max_workers` is the one whose meaning it changes — the file ships one key per step rather than a schema up front, since a key that parses and does nothing is a lie about what the workspace controls.

### Step 18 — Worker startup at scale ✅

**Delivers** a worker whose startup cost is proportional to its own task rather than to the whole eval set, and the measured figure a wide run is judged by.

- **Scope.** Upstream: the `@task` wrapper returns a placeholder for a task the selection does not name, matched on `(registry_name, args_hash)` at selection schema version 5. Steward: `worker_selection` emits those two facets, and the capture's peak RSS is measured, recorded on the manifest, and reported at `launch` and at `status` against the width the pour would use.
- **Refs.** config §6.1, §6.2; exec §12 item 5, §13 q3; sched §2.3.
- **Done when** a worker on a several-hundred-task manifest constructs one task's dataset, and a launch says what the fleet is about to cost.

**Delivered** upstream `_eval/eval_set_pruning.py` and a restructured `@task` wrapper (`tests/test_eval_set_pruning.py`, 17 cases, 8s), and Steward's `_evalset/cost.py`, two facets on the wire, and a projection line on `launch` and on `tend`.

**The measurement is the argument for the step, and it was worth running before writing anything.** A 51-task fixture with one large dataset: un-pruned peak RSS 0.33 → 0.39 → 0.52 GiB as the heavy tasks went 1 → 2 → 4, and pruned it stayed flat at ~0.27 GiB. The flat line is the whole claim. Step 17's unbounded defaults are what made it urgent — a five-hundred-task sweep was constructing five hundred copies of the un-pruned manifest, where the old ceiling of ten had bounded it to ten.

**The interception point is not where the design pass put it, and the reason generalizes.** For `eval_set(tasks=[mbpp(), gsm8k()])` the tasks are constructed while evaluating `eval_set`'s *arguments*, so nothing inside `eval_set()` runs early enough to prune anything. The `@task` wrapper reads `INSPECT_EVAL_SET_SELECTION` from the environment itself. The same ordering fact decides the recovery: by the time the boundary can react, the caller's own `tasks` list holds placeholders, so undoing a bad decision means *materializing* them, not resolving again.

**Two things the design pass got wrong.** The facet is `registry_name`, not `name`: `Task.name` is the registry name only until a task passes `Task(name=…)`, which happens inside the body pruning runs before — so a `name`-keyed pruner would have pruned every renamed task, including selected ones. And the planned resolver-level second pass is redundant, because the resolver reaches a task through `task_create`, which *is* the decorated wrapper; the only thing it could have added, skipping the module import, is unobtainable. Both are recorded as tests rather than as prose.

**And one the build got wrong, which review caught.** Pruning applied to every `@task` call in the process, so a task whose body composes another — `@task def easy(): return base("easy")` — had its inner call pruned while it was itself selected. The result is the worst shape available: an empty dataset under a perfectly matching identifier, because the identifier is computed from name, arguments, solver plan and model and never from the dataset. Pruning is now confined to the outermost construction. What remains is a real limit rather than a bug: a definition whose task bodies depend on one another's side effects is not safe to prune, nothing can detect that from outside, and `INSPECT_EVAL_SET_NO_PRUNE` is the answer. Stated in config §6.2 as an assumption pruning makes of a definition.

**The second review round produced the more interesting correction, and it was to a justification rather than to code.** The construction guard was thread-local, with a docstring explaining that a process-wide counter "would suppress pruning wherever threads overlapped" — which is under-pruning, the direction this module is explicitly allowed to fail in. Thread-local fails the other way: an inner task built through a `ThreadPoolExecutor` sees depth zero and is pruned. Same line count, opposite failure direction, and the wrong one chosen with the right one's cost as the reason. It is now a process-wide counter under a lock, with the threaded shape as a second row of the composition test — verified to fail against a simulated thread-local guard, since a test for a case nobody writes is worthless if it cannot discriminate.

**And the limitation got a home, which is what the review was really asking for.** The choice was between leaving it internal, stating it as a protocol precondition, carrying capture's per-task sample count on the wire so a worker could check itself, opting pruning in per definition, or putting the dataset in the identifier. The last two are non-starters — runner-set opt-in is consent theatre since Steward would always set it, and a dataset-sensitive identifier breaks resume on any legitimate dataset change. The sample-count check is the real alternative and was declined: it buys an earlier, more universal form of a check Steward already runs at observation time (`SHORT`), at the price of a permanent field on an `extra="forbid"` format. What landed is the precondition, stated on the surface a runner author actually reads — `eval_set_selection.py`'s module docstring and the `registry_name` field itself, so that *emitting the facets is the assertion*.

**The interim guard became a measurement, which is what made it worth keeping.** sched §2.3 asked for "a launch-time check against a measured figure" for the interval before pruning landed. The figure was already being paid for: capture executes the definition and constructs every task including datasets — the identical startup work an un-pruned worker does — so its peak RSS *is* the per-worker cost, sampled from a `Popen` rather than invented. `resource.getrusage(RUSAGE_CHILDREN)` is the tempting shortcut and is wrong: it is a running max over every reaped child, so under Hawk it reports on `uv pip install`. Because it is measured rather than assumed, it survives pruning instead of being removed by it — the number simply gets small.

**`MANIFEST_VERSION` deliberately did not move for `capture_rss`.** The manifest's version check is exact equality, so bumping it makes every committed manifest unreadable mid-run. That check exists to catch schemas whose fields would be *guessed at*; an additive optional field whose absence means "not measured" is not one.

**Nothing here fails loudly, which is the thing it had to be designed against.** Pruning can only under-fire, so a Steward that stops emitting the facets or an inspect that stops honouring them costs time and reports nothing at all. Every test therefore asserts an artifact — a body that did or did not run, a placeholder in the resolved list, two fields in the document Steward writes — and none of them measures a duration.

> ### ▸ Gate M2 — run a sweep
>
> A manifest runs as one process per task, with crash isolation and real CPU parallelism. Logs land, nothing is lost, a human reads `status`. It notices nothing: errors are counts, not anomalies. ([roadmap.md](roadmap.md) §3.1)

**Passed.** Fifteen paced tasks against `mockllm/model` in a scratch workspace, `tend_interval: 60s`, everything else default — so a process per task and no ceiling on either knob. Three tasks carried twenty-thousand-sample datasets and ran one sample each, which is the shape that separates construction cost from run cost. One task's solver raised. One worker was killed with `kill -9` forty seconds in.

**The claim that had no other coverage is the one worth recording.** Each task body appended its own name to a file named for the process that constructed it, and the result is the whole of step 18 demonstrated through Steward's own spawn path rather than through inspect's test suite: **one file with fifteen lines — the capture — and seventeen files with exactly one line each**, one per worker including both respawns, together covering all fifteen tasks. Without pruning every one of those seventeen would have carried fifteen. Resident memory agreed: light workers held 180 MB, the three heavy ones 203 MB, and the launch's bound of *at most 0.2 GiB per worker* was both correct and tight — the heavy datasets existed in exactly the three processes that owned them.

**The failure paths behaved better than the gate asks.** The killed worker's fourteen siblings ran on untouched; the next tend reaped it and respawned it, and its half-written log was preserved as `…-recovered.eval` rather than discarded, so *nothing is lost* held even for the crash. The erroring task was tried twice and then **escalated rather than looped** — `has stopped making progress after 2 attempts and will not be respawned` — which is the right M2 behaviour: it does not diagnose, but it does stop and hand the decision to a person. The run converged to fourteen complete and one needing a person, and a further tend was a no-op.

**The gate earned its keep by finding two defects that the suite could not have.** `launch` printed the startup-memory line and then echoed a turn that printed it again — identically, because both derive the width from the same manifest task count, so the launch's own copy was unconditionally redundant. It is gone, and pinned by a test asserting a *count* rather than a presence, since presence was already true. And `tend_interval` was a supported key that `steward init`'s `_steward.yaml` never mentioned while `docs/directives.qmd` documented it — the one key this gate needed, findable only by reading source. Both are the kind of thing that survives a green suite and dies on first contact with a person.

**One observation, deliberately not a defect.** The credential check refused to arm because the shell held `CLAUDE_CODE_MESSAGING_TOKEN`, which is an unrelated tool's variable matched by the `_TOKEN$` suffix. That is the check working as designed — it is a *diff* between this shell and a scheduled one, not a judgement about what an eval needs, and `_timer/env.py` argues at length that guessing at a required list would be worse. The cost is that a developer shell makes `--no-env-check` reflexive, which erodes a check that exists to prevent an all-night failure. Recorded rather than acted on: the alternative is the one the module already rejected.

## 4. Observability and tuning

### Step 19 — The tend summary and the collection queue ✅

**Delivers** the most-executed interface in the system.

- **Scope.** The summary restructured into three sections — decisions, the run, what happened — with the ordering, the all-clear states, and the live-only block. The journal position and the collection cursor. `collect` and `raise`. `ack` moving an item rather than hiding it.
- **Refs.** agent §2.2, §4, §5, §8; workflow §5.6.
- **Done when** a person reads what needs them first and in full, and an agent that has surfaced a human's decision stops being shown it.

**The design pass settled this, and the shape it arrived at is not the one this entry started with.** Both halves survive but they were applied to the wrong things.

**Items are a set with a per-item lifecycle; history is a stream with a cursor — and the cursor must not touch items.** An earlier draft had `collect` show every open decision regardless of position, which reads as safe and is not: a human-owned item sits in the agent's queue at every collection all night, because the agent surfaced it at 1am and only a person can close it. Items therefore carry a third state between open and closed — **raised**, meaning the agent has done its part — and `raise` is the verb that records it. Re-entry needs no expiry rule, because an item's id already encodes the instance rather than the condition.

**That also collapses the read/acknowledge split this entry called load-bearing.** The property it protected — a notice cannot be consumed by reading it — is now enforced *structurally*, since only an explicit act removes an item from the queue and the journal is append-only besides. `collect` advances by default; `collected` carries a position and nothing else, because an event asserting two things at once will eventually be read as the wrong one.

**The summary's job is to surface what a person must decide**, which is what fixes the section order: decisions in full at the top (the M2 gate found them buried under the task table), then every task, then what happened. *What happened* is complete rather than a delta, which keeps the summary **stateless** and leaves the cursor one job. Its admission test is *it happened to the run rather than as the run*, with high-volume classes as counts.

**Two things arrive that need no upstream work and one that will not be built.** Memory and CPU come from the process-table sweep liveness already runs; refusals and HTTP retries come from the control channel and are **live-only**, since inspect persists neither — so they describe running tasks and are labelled as such rather than being accumulated into a total, which would have required exactly the state statelessness just removed.

**Delivered** `_tend/history.py`, `_cli/collect.py`, `_cli/raise_item.py`, `_cli/items.py`, `_worker/usage.py`, `_util/size.py`, and the two journal folds — 31 new cases across `tests/schedule/test_collect.py`, `tests/workspace/test_journal.py`, `tests/schedule/test_progress.py`, `tests/worker/test_usage.py`, and one rendering case in `tests/schedule/test_tend_cli.py`. 766 tests, no launches added.

**A position is a read-time line number, not a `sequence` in the envelope, and the reason is a race the design pass did not see.** A stored sequence has to be computed from the last one, so an append would first have to read — and the run claim serialises tends but not `steward ack`, which a person types *while* a tend is in flight, because that is exactly when they are reading. Two appenders would mint the same number. A line number needs no write path at all and is stable under damage besides: a torn line keeps its number and stays counted, where an index into the parsed events silently renumbers everything after it. It rides in the extras under a dunder-flanked key so `Event.payload` can hide it from every caller that iterates a payload. workflow.md §5.6 is corrected.

**One renderer with a filter, and the filter is two flags rather than a second function.** `status_markdown(for_agent=…, since=…)` is the whole of the agent's projection. A second renderer is how two views of one run come to disagree, which step 14 already recorded as a mistake made once — and the temptation here was real, since the agent's view differs in two places rather than one.

**The rule that makes the filter safe is that no omission is silent**, and it needed its own test rather than a note. A shortened decisions list reads as an empty one, so anything set aside is replaced by a *counted* line: `2 raised, awaiting a person`, `142 earlier, not shown — --since 0 for all`. A test asserting only absence would pass against precisely the version this rule exists to rule out, so the count is asserted alongside it.

**The live block is per process for cost and per task for tallies**, which is one distinction rather than an implementation detail. A packed worker reports a row per task and every row names the same pid, so memory summed per row multiplies one process's resident set by its batch size. Refusals and HTTP retries really are per task and do sum — and both are **live-only**, so the caveat travels with the figures in one shared constant rather than being written twice. CPU is cumulative `cpu_times()` over wall clock since `create_time()`: `cpu_percent()` measures an interval, and `status` can neither block for one nor hold state between invocations.

**`raise` on something already raised appends rather than being refused**, which the tests forced a decision on. The item is still open, so it is still nameable — and chasing a decision a second time is real work whose note is worth recording. The fold keeps the last word, and the command says which of the two it did. The *already raised* branch of the failure message consequently only fires for an item that is no longer open at all, and now says so.

**The document has three `##` sections and the task table is a `###` under one of them.** A fourth top-level heading reads as a fourth thing to attend to, and an agent is required to relay the whole document — so the heading level is part of the interface rather than formatting.

**A manual pass found nothing this time**, which is worth recording only because the M2 gate found two defects a green suite had not. Three paced tasks under a real launch, driven through `status`, `collect`, `raise`, `ack`, and a second `collect`: the live block reported `3 tasks · 0 refusals · 0 HTTP retries · 0.8 GiB across 3 processes · 0.3 cores`, the block gave way to the startup bound when the fleet exited, and the acked signoff moved into *what happened* with its reason.

**One thing deferred with the verb that will resolve it.** A run whose `signoff_ready` item has been acked reads `✅ nothing needs you` rather than *accepted*. That is honest — nothing is outstanding — but the terminal *signed off* state belongs with `steward signoff` at step 26, and inventing a fourth verdict for the acked-reminder case before the attestation exists would be a state with no producer. *(Resolved at step 26, and the deferral was the right call for a reason it did not anticipate: the acked-reminder case stopped existing. `signoff_ready` became unacknowledgeable the moment there was a verb to run, so `Verdict.SIGNED_OFF` has exactly one producer — a signature — rather than two states meaning roughly the same thing.)*

**Review found three defects, and the first two share a shape worth naming: an identity or a permission taken from the nearest available value rather than from the one the design named.**

`raise` gated on `acknowledgeable` alone, so `steward raise unreadable` succeeded — and the result is worse than a no-op, because raising takes an item out of the agent's queue *without closing it*. An agent-owned item raised that way is stranded: open forever, gone from the one list that would have returned it, and owned by nobody who is looking. Now gated on `Owner.HUMAN`, with a refusal that names the verb that does apply. The generalisation is that `raise` and `ack` are not two strengths of the same act — one records a hand-off and the other a disposal — so *which items each accepts* is decided by owner and never by lifecycle.

`signoff_ready` was keyed on `definition_hash`, which is what the file hashes to **now**. Accepting a finished run and then merely saving an edit re-opened the acceptance, with no new results behind it. It is keyed on the committed manifest's `content_hash` now, which changes at a launch — which is exactly when there is a different set of results to accept. `drift` keeps the live hash, correctly: that item *is* about the file having changed. The two hashes sat one field apart on the same result and only one of them was ever right for either item.

The third is a summary contradicting its own side effects. *What happened* was projected from the journal read taken **before** the actions ran, so a turn that archived a log wrote `status.md` saying nothing had ever been done to the run, and the entry surfaced only when a later turn happened to read the file again. The turn now re-reads the journal after acting, on the turns that actually wrote to it — flagged by `_Acted.journalled` rather than inferred from `archived`, so a future action that appends and forgets is a visible omission rather than a silent one. Its rendering also said *a orphaned log*; the reason now follows the noun, since it is a bare adjective from a growing vocabulary and choosing an article per word is a rule the next reason has to remember.

**A second review round found five more, and the largest was a scope claim rather than a bug: worker crashes and respawns were listed as shipping and were not there at all.** Nothing appends a journal event for a reap, and `observation` is excluded from history by design — so a task that died at 1am and was picked up again produced no entry, which is precisely the flagship case *what happened* exists for. It is recorded now, and the narrowing is the admission test doing its job rather than a saving: **only a departure that left work unfinished**, since a worker exits at the end of every task and recording each one would put a line in the history per task that completed. *Never started* stays distinct from *died*, because a spawn that did not take and a process that went away mid-task send a reader somewhere different. The payload carries the display key beside the identifier, because an entry naming a two-hundred-character identifier is an entry nobody reads. The respawn is deliberately not recorded beside it: Steward converging is the normal thing it does, and the case where it stops has a `stalled` item already.

**The signoff key moved again, and the second answer was better than the first.** Keying on the committed manifest's `content_hash` fixed the *edit-without-launching* direction and left the other one open: `definition_hash` covers the top-level file and nothing else, so a Flow spec relaunched with different arguments, or one whose imported module changed, produces a different task set behind a byte-identical hash — and the old acknowledgment silently covers results nobody has looked at. It keys on `manifest_digest` now, a hash over the committed manifest's sorted task identifiers. That is the right answer for both directions at once, because an identifier is already Steward's notion of task identity everywhere else: two manifests naming the same work are the same result set whatever the files did.

**The live block conflated two figures that fail independently.** Refusals come from a worker's control socket; resident memory comes from the kernel. Filtering to workers that answered before reading the process table meant a fleet where every worker was mid-generate rendered as *nothing is running* — falling back to a startup projection while eight processes held real memory. Usage is now measured over every pid the in-flight record believes is alive, which also covers a worker that has not bound a socket yet, and the tallies stay restricted to what answered, with the rest **counted** as `N not answering` rather than folded into a zero. Same rule as everywhere else in this step: a figure nobody could measure must not be rendered as a measurement of nothing.

**`status.md` reported the previous tend's age.** `since_tend` is read before the turn and the new observation is written after the projection, so the file said `As of <now>` and `tended 10m ago` on one line — and on a first turn omitted the age entirely for a run being tended as the reader looked. An executed turn renders *tended just now*; a `status` renders the recorded age, which is correct there for exactly the same reason.

**And one of the new tests was nondeterministic**, which is worth recording because it was written to demonstrate pid deduplication and asserted something else on the way: that two reads of this process's RSS are equal. They are not — the test process allocates between the calls, and the full suite duly caught it at 128401408 ≠ 128466944. The process *count* was always the claim; the memory equality was a second assertion nobody needed and the only one that could fail.

**A third round found two more, and the first moved the signoff key a third time — because *result set* has a size as well as a shape.** `task_identifier` covers the solver plan, config, roles and limits and pointedly **not** `samples` or `epochs`, so that raising either leaves existing logs resumable rather than orphaning them. Steward depends on exactly that property elsewhere: `observe` computes `samples × epochs` separately and calls a short log `SHORT`. Which means a ten-sample run relaunched for twenty is the *same* identifier and a genuinely different set of results, and a digest over identifiers alone let the first acceptance cover the second in silence. `manifest_digest` hashes identifier, sample count and epochs per task. Verified against a real relaunch: `signoff_ready:68ad2f3e3304` accepted at twelve samples, `signoff_ready:f9fe2c538bdd` after alpha was widened to sixteen.

Three attempts at one key is worth naming as a pattern rather than as three mistakes. Each answer was the nearest available value that was *correct for the case in front of it* — the live hash, then the captured hash, then the identifiers — and each was wrong for a case that was not. The question the key actually answers is *what set of results is this*, and nothing about a definition answers it; only the committed enumeration does, including how much of each task it asks for.

**The second is the same class as the archive-article fix: a rendering that states something the code does not know.** Every non-`COMPLETE` observation counted as unfinished work, so a worker of a *removed* task departing was reported as being picked up again — while the same turn archived its log and nothing ever respawned it. Narrowed to `MISSING`/`INCOMPLETE`, which is *wanted and not done* rather than merely *not complete*. Reviewing that turned up the same defect one line further on, unreported: the retry clause was unconditional, so the departure on the turn the stall guard **gives up** also promised a retry. It is now taken from the turn's own spawn decisions, so the clause appears only where a replacement actually started — a hiccup and a give-up read differently to somebody working through a night, and they were rendering identically.

### Step 20 — Human interaction in a detached worker ✅ upstream 12 + 13

**Delivers** the one thing a detached worker cannot do for itself: reach a person.

- **Scope.** Reading the pending interaction out of the control channel's sample row; the parked worker as an in-flight condition `reconcile` neither reaps nor replaces; **step 14's item, kind `parked`** — owner fixed to the human and never policy-routed, carrying the `inspect acp` command built from the per-pid ACP discovery file as its resolving action; the latched `stopped` notification, which is the first one Steward sends itself; how much of the request to render.
- **Refs.** exec §7.4, §12 items 12–13, §13 q13; workflow §8, §11.1, §12; agent §2, §2.2, §6; sched §2.2.
- **Done when** a worker parked on an approval appears in `status.md` as blocked with a command that attaches to it, reconcile leaves it alone, and answering it through that command lets the run continue.

**A park is a state, not an anomaly, which is why this is here and not in Judgement.** With the agent barred from answering, there is nothing to rule on and nothing to group — the work is detection, surfacing, and one notification. Placed after step 19 because the summary is what it feeds, and because the notification half rides step 24.

**Both upstream items landed together in the local checkout**, along with the pid-keyed socket path that turning ACP on unasked made necessary rather than optional (exec §12 item 12). Unmerged upstream, like the version-5 selection facets steps 8–18 depend on.

**The bind was made to degrade and then made to raise again, which is worth recording as a reversal.** The first cut wrapped `AcpServer.start()` the way the control server wraps its own — log, tear down, yield `None` — on the argument that a channel nobody asked for must not be able to kill a fleet. The argument assumes a fallback, and for a detached worker there is none: falling through to a display that does not exist and a closed stdin is the `EOFError` this step exists to remove, now arriving hours later instead of at startup. So it raises, unqualified. What made that affordable was fixing the one environmental cause at its source — the default path was 101 of `sun_path`'s 104 bytes and is now 69 — rather than absorbing it downstream. The general form: a guard that tolerates a failure is only honest when the degraded state is one the caller can actually use.

**The design's account of *why* a park is invisible was wrong, and the correction is the reason the implementation looks as it does.** This entry and exec §7.4 both said a park is *camouflaged*: the stage's pending `ToolEvent` makes it read as a slow tool call. It does not. `call_tool` awaits `apply_tool_approval` and records the event only once the approval resolves, so during a parked approval there is **no pending event of any kind** — the sample reads as one that has gone quiet, not as one that is busy. The first live park is what said so: the item rendered `is waiting on an approval` with no tool name, because there was no `ToolEvent` to borrow one from. The corrected fact is worse for the reader and cheaper to fix — nothing to disambiguate, only something to say.

**Two integer counters became a list of records, and that one change answered three separate defects.** A counter says *that* a sample is waiting and nothing else, so the row had to borrow its `detail` from the first pending `ToolEvent` — a different call whenever several run in parallel, and no call at all in the case above — and had no start time, so a duration would have measured the sample's age rather than the wait's. `ActiveSample` now holds `PendingInteraction(kind, subject, started_at)`, one per wait. The generalisation: a count is the right shape only when the thing counted has no identity, and a human decision very much has one.

**The record belongs to the dispatch, not to the surface serving it.** It was first written inside the two ACP shims, which is where the park is — and that reports the wait only for evals running with `--acp-server`, leaving a panel or console approval as silently idle as before. It sits around the whole `ACP → panel → console` chain in `human_approver.approve` and `request_input` now. A wait is a fact about the sample; which surface happens to be asking is not part of it.

**A park forced `raise` and `ack` apart at last.** Step 19 gated `raise` on `acknowledgeable and owner is HUMAN`, and a park is human-owned and *un*acknowledgeable — so under that gate the agent could neither ack it nor raise it, and it would be offered back at every collection all night. The `acknowledgeable` half was standing in for ownership anyway. `Item` now carries `addressable` separately, and the two verbs finally accept items on their own terms.

**`Level.BLOCKING` turned out to be precedence rather than a verdict**, which made the code agree with a docstring it had been contradicting. One park among twenty running tasks is a run that is working with a decision inside it; `verdict()` returned 🛑 for it. The stopped test is now *effective* progress — unfinished work, nothing to spawn, and `running - parked <= 0` — so twenty of twenty is 🛑 and one of twenty is ⚠️ with the park first in the human's section.

**`reconcile` needed nothing, which was worth proving rather than asserting.** A parked worker's process is alive, so it resolves as `running`, holds its slot and suppresses a respawn; `_stalled` is consulted only for a task that needs spawning. Shipped as a pinning test, because the consequence is load-bearing: enough parked workers stall the fleet, and that is the intended behaviour rather than a bug someone should later "fix".

**The `stopped` notification moved to step 24**, which owns the Apprise wiring and the setting — and it cost nothing to defer, because an item id that is stable while a task is parked and gone once somebody answers is exactly the appeared/resolved diff step 24 fires on. It needed no latch of its own in the end: the item diff *is* the latch.

### Step 21 — The tuning loop ✅

**Delivers** concurrency that adapts over the night rather than being fixed at launch.

**The design pass reversed scheduling.md §3.5, and the entry's own owner split dissolved with the doctrine.** The old ruling — `tend` reports, the agent decides — accepted in writing that nothing ramps while no agent is in session, and that cost stopped being acceptable on its own terms: undershoot compounds, and the unattended hours are the ones Steward exists for. **Nothing was needed upstream, which the design pass had to prove rather than hope.** `GET /tasks/{task_id}/config` already carries the `max_samples` knob with `in_use`, the effective sandbox limiters, and every adaptive controller with timestamped `recent_changes` — so "any scale-downs since my last turn" is a comparison, per task, in the read `live.py` already makes. **Up is gated on everything; down is gated on nothing — the ratchet as code.** A +20 step costs a whole clean window: limiter saturated (§3.4's precondition), zero scale-downs, retries and errors flat, worker CPU delta under 75% of a core, twenty minutes since the task's last step, the fleet-wide sandbox budget holding, no hold. CPU had to be **windowed** to gate anything: per-pid cumulative seconds ride each observation and the inter-tend delta is the measure, because the since-start average step 19 shipped is too sluggish to catch a process arriving at saturation.

**The sandbox division died here, replaced by a sum-cap that owed no corner cases.** `max_sandboxes` is machine-wide, so the enforcement is Σ setpoints of sandbox-reporting tasks + step ≤ budget (the definition's `options` value, else the largest limit workers report), re-checked at every decision. The fold also carries last-step times, so spacing survives the process that did the stepping.

**A review pass afterwards found four unit errors in the same family, all of them the loop reading a process-scoped quantity as if it were task-scoped.** The storm cut clamped the ceiling to the *sum* of the controllers' current limits, but `--max-connections` writes one number onto each of them — so a cut of two controllers at 40 set both bounds to 80, a cut that was really a raise, and packed rows reporting a shared controller could suppress it entirely; it now clamps to the highest. `ramp hold <identifier>` did not stop that process's ceiling from being raised, since only the fleet hold reached `_ceilings`; a hold on any row now holds its process, which is the only reading the knob's scope allows. A packed row's scale-downs were narrowed to its primary model, which silently dropped every *role* model's pushback from the whole process — nobody claims the grader's controller, so nobody saw it cut; an unclaimed controller is now charged to every row, failing closed exactly where attribution is impossible. And the sandbox check reserved a whole `RAMP_STEP` before computing a step that `min(ceiling, …)` might shorten, stranding a task one partial step below a bound both numbers allowed. Six cases, one per fix plus the clamp above.

**A second pass found six more, and the shape of them is worth recording too: every one was a place where a quantity had a second meaning nobody had written down.** The sandbox sum charged only the workers that *answered* — and `busy` is the ordinary state of a fleet mid-generate, not a failure, so a routine unread worker's samples silently left the machine-wide total. `--max-samples` was implemented as a one-turn flag like its two siblings, but unlike them it decides a *regime*: a pin that lapsed left the next tend climbing a level nobody was ramping and spawning the queue at the ramp's floor, so it is now recorded and re-read, released by writing a `samples_ramp` range. The respawn clamp had a live half nobody had clamped, where a task above a narrowed ceiling sat there indefinitely and could even propose *raising* the envelope the human had just lowered. The retry gate tolerated a retry per sample slot while three documents claimed retries had to be "flat" — resolved by naming the real rule (`RETRY_GATE`, a quarter of a slot) and correcting the prose rather than pretending to a zero-tolerance the signal cannot support. `ramp hold <typo>` printed success and held nothing. And `ConfigView.persisted`, which exists precisely to separate *applied live* from *written to the eval log*, was dropped on the floor — so a retune could land while quietly losing one of the three records the ramp promises. 886 tests.

**The honest limit moved, and in the right direction.** The prediction above conflated the two paths: growth needs the *absence* of 429s, which `mockllm` provides for free — what it cannot produce is pushback. So the end-to-end growth path *is* tested, with one launch and one trick: 45 samples parked on `approver: human` approvals hold the sample semaphore, giving saturation without pushback, and the test watches the first step land (40→60, live read-back, journal action, ceiling raised to the target). The ramp-*down* path is table-level only, noted in testing.md.

## 5. Judgement

### Step 23 — Anomalies, proposals, and precedent ✅

**Delivers** errors as structured state with a lifecycle, rather than counts.

- **Scope.** The three levels — instance, class computed from exception type plus raising frame, and proposal grouped by the agent across classes. The state machine and the fold. The window closing on a ruling rather than on a clock. Per-class ruling records, so a bad grouping is recoverable. Precedent lookup and how it travels. Ruling versus policy. **What a ruling decides** — one of the four dispositions for an errored sample (re-run, excluded, zero, scored on its contents), which is the field that connects an anomaly to the number it moves. **Produces step 14's item, kind `anomaly`**, and owns the case that makes owner a function of state rather than kind: the agent's while it needs grouping, the human's once there is a proposal to answer.
- **Refs.** workflow §12, §12.1, §12.2, §12.8, §6.1, §15; sched §5.1, §5.3; exec §6.7, §6.8.
- **Done when** synthesized error populations produce stable classes across tends, a ruling on a proposal produces correct per-class records and closes exactly the right window, precedent surfaces on a recurrence, and a ruling that excludes a class is visible in what the run reports rather than only in the journal.

**A ruling has to name a disposition, and the default one is the trap.** Exclusion happens by itself — an errored sample carries no score, so it is already out of every metric and the n moved with nobody deciding it should (exec §6.8). So *no ruling yet* and *ruled: exclude* produce the same number today and mean opposite things, which is precisely the confusion the anomaly lifecycle exists to remove. Two of the four dispositions have no way to be written into a landed log at all (exec §12 item 18), so what this step can build is the **record** — the ruling says which disposition, the journal holds it, and reporting applies it — while the log stays honest about not knowing. Worth having the shape right here even though the writing-it-down half waits on upstream, because the alternative is discovering at step 26 that signoff attests to a number nobody chose.

**The three levels are one data model, so they are one step.** The seam between them is real — instance and class are computed, proposal is the agent's judgement — but it runs *through* the model rather than between two of them, and building the halves separately would mean designing the same thing twice and getting the second attempt subtly out of line with the first.

### Step 24 — Notification ✅

**Delivers** the channel that reaches an absent human.

**Taken ahead of step 23, which is a reordering.** The note below already argued *before adjudication rather than after*; dependencies agree, since the triggers are set arithmetic over step 14's item ids and step 14 landed. Steps 23 and 25 are near-inseparable and are better taken as one conversation.

- **Scope.** The six kinds and which four are Steward's alone; Apprise wiring and per-family rendering; `notification` as a Steward setting in all three spellings; what a failed notification does. Plus the triggers, which step 14 computed and discarded: an item **appearing**, the list **emptying**, tasks **finishing**, and the gate — all set arithmetic between turns, which is what makes them additive rather than count-based. **Consumes the envelope; produces no kind of its own.** Post assembly is verdict line, then what changed, then the table.
- **Refs.** workflow §11, §11.1–11.4, §5.9; agent §7.
- **Upstream.** ⚠ item 7 is **withdrawn**. `build_apprise` / `init_apprise` stay private and are imported — the same reach-around Steward already does for `_eval.evalset`, `_control.discovery`, and `_cli.main`. What is wanted is the credential discipline, not a second copy of the URL parsing, and one import buys it without asking upstream to widen a surface for one caller. `apprise` becomes a proper dependency rather than an extra.
- **Done when** each kind fires from a synthesized condition, and one manual pass lands in a real Slack workspace — the dialect decisions are the half a stub cannot check.

**One post per turn, at most, and every trigger contributes to its body.** A turn is one moment and a reader wants one message about it, so the kinds have a precedence — gate, stopped, attention, clear, progress — rather than one post each. Every post opens with the verdict line, which is also its title, so the last message in the channel is true modulo the reader's own actions. Additions are set-based rather than count-based: one item resolved and another arriving in the same tend must not read as no change. Batching is automatic, because the tend is the clock and the diff is per-tend.

**The clock trigger was dropped, and `progress` took its place.** A periodic digest needs a period, and inventing a cadence key beside the tend interval would be a second scheduler. Tasks finishing is the thing a digest was mostly carrying, and it already has an edge to fire on — a diff over the completion set, which the observation now records for the same reason it records item ids.

**The channel is a `_steward.yaml` key, which reverses workflow §5.9's original row.** A scheduled tend inherits almost no environment, so a channel that lives only in a variable is one the 02:00 turn does not have. The value never travels: nothing prints it, and the propagated copy has the key replaced. It is the second carve-out from the overrides table after `log_dir`, and it reaches the fleet two ways at once — exported, *and* as the `notification` override in each worker's selection, without which the fleet is silent while Steward posts.

**A tend that raises must still notify.** `steward.log` (step 22) covers a *transient* failure, but a permanent one — a malformed `_steward.yaml`, an unreadable log directory, expired credentials — fails identically every interval and is otherwise silent forever. So the notification path must not depend on the parts that can fail, and it is the one post with no diff to latch on: a `notified` journal event holds it to one message per distinct reason, released by the next turn that runs.

### Step 25 — Adjudication actions, and the stuck sample ✅ **done**

**Delivered** (2026-08-31) the executor and the ladder: `_tend/rulings.py` (warm requeue, landed invalidation, policy rulings from `preauthorized:`, the dispositions fold) with `_anomaly/applied.py` as its per-target memory; the stuck detector off `last_activity_at` and its item; reconcile's ruling forgiveness and authorized-first partition; the errored-cell split and the "Scores are over n of m" line. `tests/anomaly/test_rulings.py` (offline, the warm half against a recorded `requeue_sample`), `test_rulings_live.py` (the one launch — invalidate-and-resume end to end, one capture and two workers, ~21s), `test_applied.py`, plus additions across the fold, reconcile, items, live-read, ctl, and directives suites. A same-day review round hardened four seams, each with a pinning regression: the ruled population is the window's own refs (`Anomaly.refs`, rebuilt from its `instance` events) rather than an attempt-instant predicate — a failure the ruling never saw is never re-run under it, one class-scoped decision over two generations applies once, and the dispositions report attributes per window; a policy ruling's application keys on the instant the journal actually stamped (refold from the file, never a re-synthesized `utc_now`); landed invalidation checks `sample.invalidation` per target, never the log's some-sample-was header flag; and the stuck item's agent-owned action never exceeds rung 1 (`--tool-call-id` for a multi-call sample, `sample cancel` only once a cancel was asked and ignored). A second review round closed six more, each pinned: a re-run's recorded failure joins the window's coverage under a *later* ruling (`Anomaly.failed_refs` with instants, `fold.covered_refs`) so re-ruling after `reran_failed` actually re-runs the failed sample while the authorizing ruling never re-applies to its own outcome; the stuck item's id carries an episode digest of the stuck-sample set, so an ack accepts one episode rather than the task's name forever; the ladder's `inspect ctl` lines `shlex`-quote every interpolated identifier; `preauthorized: false` parses on all three surfaces as the decline-everything off-switch (the `sync: false` shape; `true` refused as granting nothing nameable); the control-channel samples read asks `?all=true` past the 100-row default cap; and `status` previews standing grants state-if-executed on the shared path (the execute branch reuses, appends, and refolds from the file) instead of reporting an already-answered class as an open question. A third and final round closed four: a `converged` witness is written only over an authoritative census — a task with an unreadable log defers it, and `task_health` holds such a task out of recovered entirely, since both pass branches trust the census's silence; the dispositions fold maps `covered_refs` under the ruling in force, so a re-run failure a later ruling excluded counts excluded rather than undecided; the stuck item's episode digests the *un-asked* call set, so spending rung 1 on one call of several re-arms the item for the next; and `score` windows route re-run failures by evidence-task membership like task windows (the class key is task-scoped and the fresh attempt's ref is unrecognizable by census membership), so another all-zero result lands `reran_failed` instead of silently opening generation two.

**Delivers** doing something about a ruling, and about a sample that has stopped moving while nothing has failed.

- **Scope.** `invalidate_samples` plus respawn with `resume`; approved re-runs scheduled ahead of pending fresh tasks; the task-level attempt ceiling; the conversation's rules. Carrying out step 23's other three dispositions, which for now means applying them where a run is *reported* rather than writing them into a log (exec §6.8, §12 item 18). Plus the live half: a sample that has not advanced past `stuck_after` detected off the fleet read a tend already performs, **producing step 14's item, kind `stuck`** — owner the agent where `_steward.yaml` pre-authorizes the first rung of the ladder, the human otherwise — and the escalation itself, `cancel-tool-call` → `cancel` → `requeue`, carried as `inspect ctl` lines rather than as Steward commands.
- **Refs.** exec §6.5, §6.6, §7.5, §8.2; sched §5.5; workflow §15; agent §6.
- **Done when** an invalidate-and-resume cycle reuses completed samples and re-runs only the invalidated ones, and a synthesized stuck sample produces an item carrying the directive that would free it — while a parked one, and a slow-but-streaming one, produce nothing.

**The two halves are one step because they are one authority question, asked at two moments.** Both are Steward acting on the run rather than reporting it; both are the adjudicated tier of §6.3 — a ruling, or a pre-authorization standing in for one made earlier; both leave a journalled record with a reason, which is what makes the autonomy reviewable. Splitting them would mean writing the authority argument twice and getting the second copy subtly out of line with the first, which is the same reason step 23 keeps its three levels together.

**Detection is nearly free and lands here anyway**, which is worth stating because the tempting alternative is to bolt it onto step 20. The read is the one the park already walks — running rows the fleet read has in hand, `last_activity_at` beside the `activity` that says what has not finished — so the cost is a second pass, not a request. But an item nobody may act on is a nuisance rather than a signal: without the ladder and the policy that routes it, `stuck` would arrive every tend, all night, offering nothing. The detector is cheap; the thing that makes it worth surfacing is this step.

**`stuck_after` is a reporting threshold and not a limit**, and the distinction is load-bearing rather than pedantic. How long a sample may work is `working_limit`, which belongs to the definition; a workspace key that enforced its own would be a config file saying something the definition already says, which is the one thing `_steward.yaml` may never do. What Steward sets is when a person is told — the same kind of thing as `stall_after`, and named to rhyme with it. The default is deliberately generous (five hours), because the failure costs a slot rather than a run, while a false positive costs the scarcest thing in the system: somebody's attention at 3am.

### Step 26 — Signoff ✅

**Delivers** the attestation, and the end of the run.

- **Scope.** The completion criterion and the gate latch; `anomalies.md`; approval terminations; curation into `logs-archive/`; what a stopped run leaves behind. **Who commits the journal** (workflow open question 4) — assigned here and now answered: it stays the human's, and what signoff owes instead is that the record is complete and quiescent when it returns.
- **Refs.** workflow §13, §13.1, §14, §14.1, §2.2–2.4.
- **No workaround needed, and the one this step planned for was already paid for.** The supersession predicate looked like a reimplementation Steward owed, since `latest_completed_task_eval_logs` is private and exported nowhere. It had already been written twice: `ObservedLogs.current` answers *which attempt is the result*, and `_current` also asks whether an attempt answers the run's shape. Curation consumes `TaskObservation.superseded` and adds no third predicate, so there is nothing here to drift from `eval_set()`'s definition.
- **Done when** signoff refuses while anomalies are open, and curation moves rather than deletes. ✅

**Four pieces, and the one that was not in the design record is the one that mattered most.** `anomalies.md` and the gate were specified; the *status flip* was not. A log whose execution ended `error` or `cancelled` stayed that way on disk after a person accepted it, so `eval_set`, the viewer, and `samples_df` all saw wreckage where a decision had already been made — and the run respawned the task every ten minutes until the stall guard tripped. Applying an `accept` ruling on a `task:` class now amends the log's header to `success`, with the halting error moved into the acceptance metadata rather than left standing under it, and a Steward-side **acceptance latch** stops reconcile respawning what a person has accepted. Both are `task:` kinds only, and the symmetry is the correction review forced: the flip originally covered every accept kind on the argument that its guard was the log's own status, so a `limit:` or `score:` acceptance would be a no-op with a witness. That is an assumption about a run rather than a property of one — a log holding operator-killed samples can also terminate with a scorer error — and accepting `limit:operator` over one cleared a task failure nobody had ruled on. A `limit:` acceptance is a claim about the data *inside* a log, and it now touches neither the log's status nor its respawning.

**The header-only trap is the thing to remember.** `write_eval_log(log, loc, header_only=True)` rewrites only `header.json` in a `.eval` zip and leaves every sample alone; the same write *without* that argument, after a header-only read, destroys them all — the recorder re-inits clean and writes `log.samples or []`. The pair must always be stated together, and `test_accepting_a_failed_task_marks_its_log_success_and_leaves_every_sample` pins it. A `started` log is accepted without being rewritten at all, because the in-place swap would manufacture a finished header for an eval that never finished.

**What the exploration corrected before any of it was built:** an `accept` ruling's window lives in `Anomalies.settled`, not `.open`, so the executor iterating `.open` would have found nothing; three readers of *unfinished* had to move together or a latched run reads 🛑 forever and nags for a timer; and `signoff_ready` had to *stop* being acknowledgeable, which made the item filter's id-only test a migration hazard — a workspace that acked it under the old behaviour would have stayed quiet forever and never been offered the verb.

**Review found nine, and seven of them were one mistake wearing seven hats: a value read at the wrong grain, or at the wrong moment.** The signature was appended *after* the final turn had rendered and synced, so the durable snapshot — the only thing a remote reader has, and this run never tends again — said 🏁 *waiting to be accepted* forever; a second turn now runs under the same claim once the signature is in the journal. `accepted_tasks` returned a set, flattening away *which decision accepted which task*, so after a relaunch an unrelated `exclude` on the same task re-latched it and stopped its respawns under a decision nobody had made. `caveats` grouped two generations under one ruling and then kept the first, understating the scope of a class-wide decision. The caveat counts spanned *attempts* where the denominator line beneath them counted *rows*, so three samples that failed, re-ran and failed again printed *6 samples excluded* three lines under a note saying three — narrowed now to the current attempt exactly as `dispositions` narrows the errored cell, with the replaced failures reported as what they are. The rendered-file episode was keyed to neither file, so `anomalies.md` writing while `status.md` could not recorded a *restoration* and one permanent failure arrived as news every second turn. And the signature's exceptions came from the accepted windows alone while `anomalies.md` admits an acknowledged `stalled` or `unreadable` as a caveat too — so a run could report "no accepted exceptions" over its own caveat list; `caveats()` now feeds all three, which is the property that made it one function in the first place.

The other two were gaps rather than grain. The gate ignored `result.failures`, and an acceptance is exactly the case that makes those a refusal rather than a warning: the ruling settles the window and the latch subtracts the task from unfinished work, so a failed log amendment left the gate reading a settled run while a log on disk still said `error`. And `--by ""` satisfied `required=True`, signed, curated, disarmed the timer, printed a success — and `read_signoff` then discarded the event, leaving a run with no attestation and nothing left to notice. **`required` means present, not somebody.**

**A second round found four more, and named the same two shapes again.** *Grain*: a caveat's membership keyed on `sample_id:epoch`, so two tasks that each lost `s0:1` collapsed into one sample — a class key is an exception type and a raising frame and says nothing about which task raised it, which is why the scope line has always been able to read *across 4 tasks*. *Silence read as absence*: `_members` treated **the census matched none of these** identically to **there is no census**, so a class accepted on one attempt and relaunched into a clean one went on reporting the superseded log's failures as samples excluded from the data, and the next signature named it as an exception the run no longer carried. The census now answers for itself wherever it has anything to say about the class, and a decision with nothing left in the data is not a caveat. That last one forced the pair of documents together at last: `status.md` renders the same caveats one line each and had no census to compute them from, so the turn decides them **once** and both documents render what it decided — the claim `anomalies_md`'s docstring had been making about a function two callers were invoking with different arguments.

**And the fourth was a swallowed failure with two acts standing on it.** `_rerender` caught everything and returned the pre-signature turn, after which the timer came down and a success was printed — so a run whose snapshot could not be written kept saying *finished, waiting to be accepted* forever, with no scheduled turn left to repair it and nothing said to the person who had just signed. A turn that raises was not even the only way in: `_write_rendered` swallows an `OSError` by design, so a turn that returns perfectly well proves nothing about the file. It is now checked by **reading the file back and asking whether it carries this signature** — the only form of the question whose answer is not an inference about the code that was supposed to write it — and each artifact that did not catch up is named with the command that repairs it. The signature and the disarm both stand: a filesystem must not unmake a decision a person made, and a timer left armed on a signed run spends money against an explicit instruction.

**A third round found seven, and the two P1s were the gate's own doctrine applied unevenly.** The gate refuses *unnamed* holes and signs over named ones — an ack being a name — and neither half held for the two conditions an ack can name. An unreadable log was a warning, so a finished run containing one signed with **no exceptions at all**: the one hole nobody can size, unrecorded. And an acknowledged stall went on counting as unfinished forever while `anomalies.md` printed it as a caveat reading *the task's results stand as they are, without it* — with the refusal's remedy being *rule the class*, which a stall need not have, since the guard fires on attempt history rather than on an anomaly. Both are now the ordinary shape: `settled_by_decision` counts a ruling and an acknowledged stall as one thing, and an unread log refuses until somebody names it.

Three more were narrowing the round before had not finished. A `task:` or `score:` window never consulted the current attempt, so an accepted task failure that a clean relaunch replaced stayed a caveat and would have been named by the next signature. An acknowledgment names a **condition**, not an instant — *this log will not read*, *this task has stopped making progress* — and both can stop being true, so `caveats` now takes what has demonstrably *cleared*; stated that way round on purpose, because a subject it cannot place must keep its caveat, and losing a footnote nobody asked to lose is the worse of the two mistakes. And `composed_effect` counted the whole class where a class key outlives its generations: three samples settled under a prior one and two open under this one composed *5 samples excluded from scoring* over an entry scoping two, so the narrowed population is now carried as refs and intersected with the windows actually being ruled.

The last two were each an assumption about what another component had already done. Curation skipped orphans because a tend archives them every turn — except that a **paused** run makes no changes to itself, and signing a paused run is allowed, so a signed directory could keep results belonging to an identifier the definition no longer names, with the timer down and nothing left to tidy up. And the previous round's `is_file()` check proved nothing: a stale `anomalies.md` from an earlier turn is a file. A turn now reports which documents it actually wrote (`TendResult.rendered`), which is the only form of the question whose answer is not an inference about the code that was supposed to write them.

**One doctrine changed during the build.** *An agent must never run `steward signoff`* became *an agent must never decide to*. Prompting the human when the run is ready is the agent's job, and carrying out their answer is the same act as relaying a ruling through `rule --by` — what is recorded is the name of whoever decided, so a signature stays traceable to a person whichever process typed it. The old rule was answering *who owns this decision* with a rule about *who may type*, and the two come apart the moment the person is not at the terminal.

> ### ▸ Gate M3 — walk away
>
> An overnight run tends itself, notices what matters, escalates it, and ends in an attestation. This is the product. ([roadmap.md](roadmap.md) §3.2)

## 6. Completeness and trust

### Step 27 — The sandbox budget ✅

**Delivers** a fleet that does not ask a Docker host for `workers × 2 × cores` containers.

- **Scope.** Elastic versus host-bound, read from the live config rather than a manifest field; the budget's three enforcement points — spawn clamp, over-budget cut, step admission; the budget carried across turns; redistribution when a worker exits.
- **Refs.** sched §3.6, §3.7; exec §12 items 9, 10.
- **Done when** the arithmetic is unit-tested at all three points, a pin is provably not clamped, and the cut is provably free to go below the ramp's floor.

**The division died and the step went with it, mostly.** Both upstream dependencies dissolved rather than landing: the `max_sandboxes` override (item 9) is landed and deliberately unwritten, and the per-task sandbox *type* (item 10) is unneeded because the live config read reports each task's effective limiters directly. What replaced the division — bounding the fleet-wide sum of sample setpoints, since a task's containers never exceed its running samples — arrived inside **step 21**, whose retrospective owns the argument. The `⚠ upstream` marking and the *No workaround* line went with the division; **k8s and unsandboxed evals were never affected**, since `k8s_sandbox` does not override `default_concurrency` and the base `None` is elastic.

**What was left over, and is the only code this step added: the sum-cap gated growth but not the starting point.** Step 21 enforced the budget where the ramp asks to climb, which is exactly one of the three places it binds — and not the one that matters first. A fleet does not climb into the multiplication; it starts there, because every worker spawns at the ramp's floor and the floor knows nothing about the host. Ten tasks at 40 against a budget of 28 is the whole 10× overshoot, reached before the ramp is consulted, whereupon the admission rule declines every step and reports a machine already over-committed. So `reconcile` gained the budget and clamps a spawn to an even share of it, `plan_tuning` gained a cut for a fleet already past it, and the tend records the budget it learned so the next spawn can use a number only a running worker can report. sched §3.6 rewritten.

**Two boundaries the new moves respect.** A **pinned** setpoint is never clamped — under a pin the level is a number a person wrote, and a pinned fleet overshooting its own `max_sandboxes` stays the contradiction §3.6 reports rather than resolves; the clamp turns on `resolve_samples_ramp` being active at all, which is `resolve_max_samples`'s own setpoint-versus-starting-point distinction. And the cut is **free to land below the ramp's floor**, which it has to be: 28 across ten tasks does not divide into 40, so a cut clamped at the floor would leave the host exactly as over-committed. It is the one move in the loop that goes under the floor, and the ramp climbs back out through the gates as tasks finish.

**The cgroup wrinkle §3.6 flagged is fixed upstream rather than worked around** — [inspect_ai#5164](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_ai/pull/5164) makes `default_concurrency()` (and the docker CLI limiter, and `max_subprocesses`) read the cgroup CPU quota and the affinity mask instead of `os.cpu_count()`, so the number Steward reads back off its workers is the pod's rather than the node's. Nothing here depends on it landing: the budget arithmetic is the same whatever the provider reports, and a wrong provider number was always going to be wrong for the eval running alone too.

### Step 28 — Online scanning in the fleet ✅

**Delivers** a scanning definition Steward can run, with scans riding the workers as samples settle and exactly one process ever touching the scan directory's lifecycle.

- **Scope.** The record-only worker contract (dispatch and buffer, never the bracket; a missing scan dir refuses loudly at startup); capture serializing the scan spec — names, `ScannerSpec`s, config hash, resolved scans location; **the scanner merge** — definition's + Steward's built-in + the operator's, injected through the selection in `ScannerSpec` form, name collisions refused at launch; `launch` laying the directory down from the merged spec before the first spawn, and the verification at re-launch — changed refused, added admitted; **`scan_model`** in three spellings, reflexive with `SCOUT_SCAN_MODEL`, reaching workers as the exported scout spelling — and unset, scanners ride the sample's own model under evaluation (then its first model role), which is what lets the built-in ride every run unconfigured; lifting `_refuse_scanners`.
- **Refs.** exec §4.2, §4.3; §12 items 20–22; inspect_ai's own `design/eval-set-scanners.md`.
- **Done when** a fleet of workers over a scanning definition lands one row per sample in one scan directory — injected scanners included, under the setting's model — and the finalize-prune hazard is pinned: a worker finishing while a sibling still runs deletes nothing.

**The design pass replaced the boundary mode before it was built.** The original trio ran scans as detached children of the tend — a third `eval_set()` mode over log locations, a drain queue, in-flight accounting — all to keep the scan directory single-writer. The settled design gets single-writer by splitting the contract where the hazards actually sit: the four §4.2 hazards all live in the lifecycle bracket and none in the rows, so workers record (upstream's own per-sample dispatch, currently rejected under selection) and the runner brackets — init at launch from a captured spec, fold and finalize in the tend, no definition ever executed for scanning. Verified in source before settling: recording requires the directory and spec to exist; `FileRecorder.sync`/`status` are static; finalize's orphan cleanup needs scanner *names* only, which `_scan.json` holds; the selection document already carries `eval_set_id`. What the mode would have cost is what this saves: a tend interval of latency on every result, the definition's startup tax per pass (minutes on Hawk), and a second detached-process family. exec §4.2–4.4 and sched §4 rewritten; `INSPECT_EVAL_SET_SCAN` withdrawn unbuilt.

**The upstream items landed and the marking came off; what is left is a version floor.** Items 20–22 are all in `inspect_ai` main — record-only selection scanning, the ambient scan-model default, `scan_init_from_spec` — and `_scan/bracket.py` calls the last of them directly. What remains is not a missing capability but a *release*: `pyproject.toml` still pins both `inspect-ai` and `inspect-scout` to git `main`, because none of it is in a published wheel yet and none of it is announced by a version number. The check is one line and is recorded beside the pin — importing `scan_init_from_spec` from the latest released wheel — and it was last run on 2026-09-01 against `inspect-ai` 0.3.261, which does not carry it (the branch runs 0.3.262.dev). When it does, the two floors rise, `[tool.uv.sources]` and the `override-dependencies` line that exists only to let it win are deleted, and the suite runs against the wheels.

### Step 29 — The scan lifecycle in the tend ✅

**Delivers** results readable mid-run, coverage visible, and the bookkeeping done by the run's one writer.

- **Scope.** The sync fold and its cadence, with the growing-buffer cost measured before it is engineered around; coverage — recorded rows against landed samples — in `status` and the table; scanner errors classed like sample errors, keyed scanner + exception type, **through step 23's kind `anomaly`** with no kind of their own; the terminal finalize after adjudication and log cleanup settle, and its idempotence across adjudication re-runs.
- **Refs.** sched §4.1–4.2; exec §4.3, §4.4.
- **Done when** rows land in `scan_results_df` within a tend of their samples settling; a worker killed between logging and scanning leaves a visible coverage gap that its respawn's resume-scan closes; and the terminal finalize prunes exactly the superseded attempts' rows.

**The two halves the fold blocks were built; the two it does not were deferred with step 30.** Nothing in the tend touched the scan directory at all — `finalize_scan` had no caller outside a test — and since workers in selection mode never enter upstream's `scan_context`, *no row was ever compacted* until somebody folded it. That is the whole of what step 30 needed, so it came first: `sync_scan` wraps `FileRecorder.sync(..., complete=False)`, which upstream documents as safe mid-scan, and the tend calls it once a turn while the run is not quiescent — `running or departed`, so the last worker's final rows are folded by the turn that reaps it and a settled campaign pays nothing. The terminal `finalize_scan` is wired at signoff, after curation, where the run is quiescent and single-writer and the prune is therefore honest; it warns rather than refusing, on `_rerender`'s grounds.

**`complete=False` carries all of the risk and is the one thing to keep stated**: the buffer is not cleaned, so a sibling worker's `is_recorded` keeps answering; orphan rows are not pruned, which is §4.2's pinned hazard; and the scan is not marked complete, so a crash leaves it resumable. The cost is that a mid-run fold re-compacts the whole buffer and so grows with the run — measured into `steward.log` as the step asked, and deliberately not engineered around, since the alternative is Steward reimplementing scout's own bookkeeping one directory away.

**The two deferred halves — coverage, and scanner errors as anomalies — closed together, and each turned out to rest on one fact found in source.**

**A scanner's exception is recoverable, but from only one of scout's three error columns.** `ResultReport.to_df_columns` writes all three and two are dead ends: `scan_error` is `str(ex)` where the message parser wants `repr(ex)`, so it never matches; `scan_error_type` is the literal string `"refusal"` on every error row regardless of what threw. `scan_error_traceback` is a real `traceback.format_exc()` and `parse_error(None, traceback)` reads it exactly as it reads a sample's. So `scanerror:{scanner}:{type}@{frame}` is the sample-error doctrine applied one layer in, and the distinction the deferral asked for is a property of the population rather than a number anybody computes: *scanning is broken* is one class spanning five hundred transcripts, *this transcript breaks the scanner* is a class of one.

**The kind forced `SAMPLE_SHAPED` apart, which is the design call worth recording.** One constant was answering two questions — *does this kind have a sample population* (four readers, all display) and *are the sample marks honest on it* (`honest`, `dispositions`) — and every kind had answered both the same way until this one. A `scanerror:` class has an instance per transcript and so a population, and its residue is a verdict that is *absent* rather than a row that is wrong: nothing to exclude, nothing to zero. So the marks meaning moved to `SAMPLE_MARKED` and `SAMPLE_SHAPED` kept membership. `rerun` is refused too, a third row in `honest()`: the eval is fine, only the reading of it failed, and Steward has no verb that re-scans. `accept` and `dismiss` are the answers — which is exactly what the acknowledgment this retires already meant.

**And the narrow surface built last session came out**, because two machines refusing over one fact is how one of them drifts. Gone: `ScanFindings.incomplete`, `TendResult.scan_incomplete`, `items.SCAN_INCOMPLETE` with its owner entry and builder, `gate.UNSCANNED` and its blocker, the `anomalies_md` ack branches, and the runbook's ack bullet. `OPEN_WINDOW` refuses it now, once.

**Coverage's numerator is free and its denominator is where the resume lives.** Every scan row already carries the log it names and the transcript it is about, and the turn already holds the basename→attempt map, so the numerator accumulates inside a pass that was already happening — *recorded once every scanner has answered*, which is upstream's own resume predicate rather than a rule invented here, and an errored row still counts because 29a's class is where that failure is reported. The denominator is the header's `total_samples`, exact for every task that was never retried. After a retry it is exactly wrong: the new log carries the samples that already succeeded under their original uuids while their scan rows still name the superseded file, so counting rows per log undercounts by the reused population and counting them all hides the re-runs nothing has scanned yet behind samples that left the results. The only stable key is the sample uuid, which no header carries — so a task pays one summaries read, gated twice (it was resumed *and* it has scan rows), and its numerator is intersected against what its current log actually holds.

**One read, two readers, and the second was a live defect.** A scan finding on a **reused** sample was being dropped from `affected_refs`, `dispositions` and `anomalies.md` for the same reason coverage would have miscounted it — the narrowing was *location equals the current log* and its location is the superseded one. The predicate moved into one place (`instances.in_results`) and became *is this sample in the current log* for scan-shaped kinds, keyed on the uuid set coverage had just read. The count and the list it summarizes now come from one read and cannot drift.

**The intersection has to include the scanners that produced nothing**, which review caught and which would have quietly falsified the whole feature. Skipping a scanner with no parquet — the shape it takes early in a run, and permanently for one added at a re-launch — takes it out of the intersection, so one scanner that answered for everything reports the run as fully covered on behalf of one that never started. An empty answer set rather than an absent one, and the same for a parquet that will not read, since unknown cannot be counted as answered.

**Coverage reports and does not refuse**, which is a decision rather than an omission. Signoff refuses over the errored half (a `scanerror:` window, like any open window) and leaves the unreached half to the column and the note, because what would close a coverage gap is scout's resume over the scan directory and Steward has no verb for it — a blocker would wedge an added-scanner run permanently, and the only escape would be the ack this step just retired.

**Recorded rather than engineered around:** upstream's `_reports_for_parse_error` emits one error row *per scanner* for a transcript that failed to parse — same exception, same traceback, fanned across every scanner — so under a scanner-keyed class that is N classes of one rather than one class of N. They share a type and frame and so read adjacent; splitting them properly needs a transcript-shaped key the design does not have.

**One caveat the fold acquires and the design should carry: it is a local-fleet capability.** Scout's buffer lives in a local user data dir, so the tend folds its own machine's workers and no others. A Hawk-dispatched worker's rows would reach the directory only at its own finalize — which is moot while Hawk rejects `scan:` locally (§8), and is the first thing to check when it does not.

### Step 30 — Scan results as leads ✅

**Delivers** scan output the agent can act on.

- **Scope.** Reporting distributions rather than verdicts; a scan result as a measurement only the agent can read; collection versus investigation; **what Steward's built-in scanner looks for** — its carriage is step 28's merge channel, its substance (the validity / behavioural / infra kinds of workflow §12.6.1) belongs here, beside the leads it produces. **Produces step 14's item, kind `scan_finding`** — owner the agent, since a distribution carries no verdict a human could answer.
- **Refs.** workflow §12.6, §12.3, §12.5; agent §1.
- **Done when** a flat distribution and an outlier distribution over the same scanner produce visibly different leads in the tend summary.

**The done-when could not be met and should not have been aimed at, which is the step's real finding.** A distribution over values Steward has no semantics for cannot be turned into a lead without a threshold, and every candidate threshold is the category error §12.6 opens by naming. What replaced it: a **boolean** scanner has already done the judging — its author wrote a question with a yes and a no — so a `true` becomes an anomaly of class `scan:{scanner}[:{label}]` and every other value type is recorded, readable, and never escalated. Steward reads a line somebody else drew rather than inventing one. §12.6 and §12.6.1 rewritten; the distribution half is deferred, with what it costs stated rather than dropped.

**And the item is not a new kind.** `scan_finding` was specified as step 14's fifteenth item kind; it turned out to be an *anomaly*, and building it as one was two enum entries and a `honest()` row rather than a vocabulary entry, a renderer branch, an owner policy and an ack rule. What came free is the whole point: `steward investigate` / `propose` / `rule`, precedent travelling across generations, escalation to the person when nobody collects, `anomalies.md` for an accepted one, and a signoff gate that already refuses on an open window. The item's owner is still the agent and its action is still `steward investigate`, exactly as specified — through the general path.

**The window opens on detection rather than on escalation**, which is the decision the rest follows from. Opening only for what survives the agent's review would make *nobody looked* and *somebody looked and found nothing* the same state, which is precisely the distinction §12.7 calls as valuable as a finding. So *looked, nothing here* is a `dismiss` ruling with a reason, and the gate holds the signature until every flag has one. The counterweight, since a dismissal leaves no caveat anywhere: the readiness item **counts the dismissed findings and the runbook tells the agent to name them**, so the operator hears *the model tried to read the grader and failed* at the moment they are asked to sign, rather than finding it later.

**Sample-shaped, so `exclude`/`zero`/`score` are honest against it** — `honest()`'s row widened from `("error", "limit")`. A score established to be wrong should not be silently averaged in, and refusing the marks would have left `accept` and `dismiss` as the only answers to one.

**The notification discipline changed with it.** A landed task carrying an untriaged flag is not one thing to say but two, and *finished cybench* standing alone for twenty minutes before *a decision needs attention* is the wrong first message. So the completion post is **held while an agent is attached** (`_unattended`, unchanged) for at most six tends, and the hold is a deferral rather than a suppression because the held task is kept out of the recorded completion set as well as out of the diff — subtracting from only the first would spend the finish and announce it never. With no agent nothing is held: the finish posts at once, and the finding escalates on the ordinary unattended horizon.

One correction this step owns: workflow §12.3's *findings arrive last* described the drain design. Findings now accumulate with the run and can fire mid-campaign; what arrives last is **investigation** — the run's tail is the agent over the results, not the scanner over the logs. The section's consequence stands unchanged: signoff still sits after scan coverage completes and anomalies settle.

One frontend caveat covering all three: **Hawk rejects `scan:` locally**, so none of this has a Hawk path until §8.

### Step 31 — `analysis.md` ✅

**Delivers** what investigation produces, per task, mirrored where the data lives.

- **Scope.** Skeleton rendering; the unprobed count; adjudicating as you go; mirroring into `log_dir` including on S3. **Produces step 14's item, kind `unwritten`** — owner the agent, one per task section still unwritten (agent §4).
- **Refs.** workflow §12.7, §12.4.
- **Done when** the file exists per task and reaches the log directory.

**One file, settled at step 30 in the design record.** `scanning.md` was retired: the split was drawn on *when* the two were written rather than on what they held, and online scanning collapsed the two moments into one — same sitting, same task, same author, and the roll-up a copy of the file above it. Scanning becomes a section of `analysis.md`, and the "looked, nothing here" entry that justified the second file is now a `dismiss` ruling carrying its reason, which the journal holds and this file quotes.

**The step is a merge, not a renderer, and that is its whole difficulty.** Every other generated document is rewritten whole every turn; this one is the only file in the workspace neither party owns. So Steward keeps a facts block current between a pair of HTML-comment markers under each task's heading, and every byte outside them comes back unchanged. Four clauses, each stopping one loss: a task with no section gets one appended; a task with a section has only the text between its markers replaced; a section whose markers **do not pair** is left entirely alone with the damage sent to `steward.log`, because guessing at a boundary in a file whose other half is work is the one unrecoverable mistake here; and a section for a task the manifest no longer names is left alone, since a removed task's investigation is still what happened. The marker is keyed on the **identifier** and the heading carries the **display key**, which is the split `anomalies_md` already makes and which means renaming a task does not orphan its section.

**A read failure is a third answer, not an empty file.** `analysis.md` is composed from nothing when absent and the write is an atomic replace — so treating a permissions error as *no file yet* would overwrite an investigation with a stub. The read therefore returns text, empty, or `None`, and `None` means nothing happens at all.

**Two more losses review caught, both of them the same mistake in different clothes.** The facts are composed near the top of the turn, because the items have to report what is unwritten — but the *write* is at the end, past the spawns, requeues, invalidations and archive moves, which on a busy turn is minutes. The claim serializes Steward against Steward and says nothing about the other author, so a merge held across that interval would atomically replace whatever they saved in it. The write re-reads and re-merges; the reported `unwritten` stays from the earlier read and is at worst one turn stale, which is a report rather than a file. And *byte-identical* was nearly true rather than true: splitting on bare lines and re-joining on `"\n"` rewrites every line ending of a CRLF file and eats whatever trailed the last one — whole-file churn arriving as a diff nobody made. `splitlines(keepends=True)` and `"".join` make the promise literal.

**Mirroring cost nothing**, as the exploration predicted: `sync.py` already carries every top-level non-dotfile, and `tests/workspace/test_sync.py` had been writing `analysis.md` by name since before the file existed.

**Two gates on the `unwritten` item that the scope did not name, and both matter.** A section arrives with a task's **first log**, not with its manifest row — a run has no numbers to explain before it has run, and appending sections at capture would put an item on every workspace from its first turn. And the item is raised only where an agent has actually **collected**, which is `Supervision.ever_armed`'s reasoning applied to the other kind of expectation: a run somebody drives by hand is owed a write-up by nobody, and raising one would park every such run at ⚠️ over work no one agreed to do. The facts block is still kept current either way — those are Steward's.

**Not acknowledgeable, not a signoff blocker, and it does move the verdict.** Writing *looked, nothing here* is the deliverable rather than something to wave past (§12.7), so there is no ack. Holding a person's attestation hostage to an agent's prose is the wrong trade (agent §6), so there is no blocker. But a finished run with the write-up still owed reads ⚠️ rather than 🏁 — two open items, *sign it* and *write it up* — which is agent §4.3 exactly: unwritten is work outstanding, not something that happened. It is also the one agent item held back from the unattended-workspace channel escalation, since waking somebody at 3am about the absence of prose is not what that path is for.

### Step 32 — Smoke gate ✅

**Delivers** the rehearsal before the sweep.

- **Scope.** Writing the `limit` override the selection document already carries; the Steward-side wall-clock cap (*not* a passed-through `time_limit`, which is in the identifier); the smoke's own log directory under `.steward/`; what a smoke failure blocks, and what a stale one warns about.
- **Refs.** workflow §7.1; exec §12 item 8.
- **Done when** a smoke run truncates, caps, and gates the real launch.

**No longer blocked upstream.** Step 16 landed the version-3 `overrides` container with `limit` in it, so both halves of the log-directory-and-slice problem are expressible for script definitions as well as Flow ones. What is left is entirely Steward-side, which is why this moved out of the upstream-gated set — and the cap was always Steward's, since `time_limit` is in the identifier and passing one through would break the matching the smoke depends on. Upstream turns out to be firmer than that: `time_limit` is in `NOT_OVERRIDABLE` and the container forbids extra keys, so passing one is unrepresentable rather than unwise.

**Built. The slice rides the workers, not the capture, and everything else follows from that.** The obvious implementation applies `limit` where `launch` applies every other override — at capture — and it is wrong twice over. A manifest enumerated under the limit reports per-task counts of two, which is a description of the rehearsal rather than of the run; and `limit` is a shaping field, so the rehearsal's `manifest_digest` would differ from the launch's for the one reason that does not matter. Capturing whole and truncating per worker makes the smoke's manifest *the same manifest* the launch will capture — which is what turns "a smoke is valid for a manifest" into a set comparison over task identifiers, and what lets the digest ride beside them. This is the sentence execution.md item 8 was written for, finally used.

**Untended, which retires step 16's leftover for this caller rather than paying it.** The cap stops workers with `inspect ctl`, whose cancelled logs land as `status="error"` and would be respawned by anything reconciling them. Nothing reconciles a smoke: it spawns once, watches the directory, stops on its deadline, folds and reports. So the per-identifier *do not start this again* record stays deferred, where `steward stop` will need it and this does not.

**One defect found by running it, and it is the reason the live test exists.** The rehearsal originally shared the run's in-flight record and workers directory. The record accounts for *spent attempts per identifier* and `reconcile` stops respawning after two — so two smokes of a two-task definition left both tasks stalled before the real launch had run a single sample. A rehearsal that makes the run it precedes unrunnable is worse than no rehearsal. Its machine state now lives inside `.steward/smoke/` with its logs, and one `rmtree` clears the lot.

**The scope widened, on the user's ask, from *did it terminate* to *is it configured the way you think*.** Three checks over the rehearsal's own transcripts — the scanners' findings, every model's effective context window, and whether reasoning is replayed to the model at both the conversation and the wire. Each catches a failure that produces an eval which completes, scores, and is wrong, which is the class a green exit code cannot distinguish from success. The context-window check is on `get_model_input_tokens` rather than `get_model_info`, and that distinction is the user's correction: providers alias an unregistered frontier name onto a known entry before any lookup, so a pre-deployment codename with no database entry gets a real window and is *fine* — a resolution-based check would have failed exactly the configuration it was meant to bless.

**Three verdicts rather than two**, because *unexercised* (a non-reasoning model has nothing to replay) and *undetermined* (a provider SDK this machine lacks) are neither passes nor failures, and collapsing either into failure would have blocked a whole category of legitimate runs on day one. A real failure blocks and can be waived by name, recorded in the journal — a gate with no escape is a gate people route around.

**The digest finally exists.** agent.md §9 has listed it among the four artifacts an agent must trust since before there was any smoke, with no mechanics anywhere. It is `.steward/smoke/digest.md`, and reading a rehearsal's whole logs to produce it is the one exception to *never read a full eval log* — held to four simultaneous bounds, and the transcript never leaves Steward.

**Six review findings after that, and four of them were one mistake repeated: the rehearsal did not rehearse the launch.** It read only whether tasks *finished*, when workers force `continue_on_fail` — so four samples landing with two errored reported ready, and a bad key, a sandbox that would not start and a scorer that threw were all green. It captured its own arguments, type and overrides instead of the committed manifest's, so a workspace launched with `-A` had its defaults rehearsed while the launch that followed reused what was committed. It ignored `--scan-model` and `--notification` and settled the scan model *after* the fleet had started, so the workers scanned with the shell's answer while the digest reported the workspace's. And its cap cancelled and read the directory in the same breath, folding a scan its workers were still writing. Sample records are now classed the way a tend classes them and any errored sample fails the rehearsal; the capture resolution lives in one function both callers pass through; the channel and the scan model are settled before the first worker; and every exit — cap, exception, interrupt — goes through one `reap` that stops the fleet and waits, bounded, for it to land.

**The other two were about what a passing smoke claims.** Coverage was keyed on task identifiers alone, which deliberately exclude the sample count, the epochs and the selection — so a dataset that doubled kept every identifier and read as rehearsed while being a materially different night. The manifest digest now rides beside them, which is possible only because the slice rides the workers; it is compared where the identifier sets are equal, so the per-task sentence still wins where it applies. And the journal fold read back to the most recent *pass*, letting a rehearsal that had just failed sit behind a passing one from an hour ago; the newest smoke is now the answer whatever it concluded.

The other three. **A second pre-existing one, found while testing the round above.** `_pool_advice` read the journal with no guard, so the same unreadable file that `_unrehearsed` had just been taught to decline politely took the whole launch down with a traceback a few steps later — after the manifest was committed, which is the worst place for it. A failed append is different again — the advice is given anyway and will be given again next launch, since the record is what makes it once-only and the advice is useful without it. What does not change is the `launched` append, which still raises: an event that did not land is a hole in the only copy.

**A sixth round asked the opposite question — had five rounds of *a zero read as a pass* left the rehearsal reporting things nobody needs reported?** Two of the three candidates were real, and both had the same shape: a rule written against the case it was reproduced on, fired on a much larger set than that case.

The scan-coverage check failed a rehearsal on **any** shortfall. A definition's scan `filter` is a SQL clause applied per sample, and a transcript it excludes is not scanned at all — no parquet row, no snapshot entry (`inspect_ai._eval.task.scan.scan_eval_sample`) — while the filter reaches Steward only as a hash inside the spec's metadata. So a partial count cannot be told from a correct one, and blocking on it fails every filtered definition on every rehearsal: the gate that fires each time is the gate nobody reads, which is the argument this step's own signoff work had already accepted two rounds earlier when it backed out the equivalent blocker there. The block is now narrowed to *nothing at all was recorded*, which a filter can still cause but only by matching none of a two-sample slice — a filter narrows which transcripts a scanner sees without stopping it running on the ones that match. Partial coverage states its number and passes.

**The premise underneath it was wrong in the other direction, and worth recording because it is the sort of thing that reads true.** *No scan rows is just a scan that found nothing* — it is not: scout appends a result for every transcript-scanner pair on success and on error alike (`_scan._scan_one`, *always append a result*), recording even a scanner that returned nothing, so a spotless review of four transcripts reads as four. A shortfall really is transcripts nobody answered for. The check counts rows, not findings, and the narrowing above rests on the filter rather than on that.

And `_unrehearsed` warned on **any** journal damage, so one line torn by one crash warned on every launch for the rest of the workspace's life, in the same words as `JOURNAL_DAMAGE` — the tend's item for it, and the one place it can actually be cleared. The hazard it was aimed at is a torn *tail*: a crash mid-append leaves its fragment where the newest smoke would be, so the fold reads the pass before it. Damage below the last readable event cannot do that — whatever it destroyed is older than something that did read, so losing it can only under-report coverage, which is the direction that warns rather than the direction that reassures. `Event.line` is counted over lines rather than over parsed events, so the two positions are comparable and a torn line does not renumber what follows it.

The third candidate was left alone: the coverage exception folded into every signature when a scanner is filtered dilutes the exceptions list, but it records a true fact and interrupts nobody.

**One pre-existing bug in `launch` fell out of the same refactor.** `reuse_overrides` was computed from the flags alone, so a `STEWARD_EPOCHS` nobody typed but somebody exported was read, resolved, and then discarded — contradicting `_prior_overrides`'s own statement that any variable replaces the committed set. It needed both halves: nothing typed *and* nothing resolved, since `--no-overrides` resolves to nothing on purpose and must displace rather than fall back.

### Step 33 — Store read and publish ✅

**Delivers** reuse across runs, and publication as an act of signoff.

- **Scope.** The read half (a cache); publication gated on the attestation, not on landing; configuration.
- **Refs.** exec §5.3–5.6; workflow §13.2.
- **Done when** publication happens at signoff and never before.

**The workers' side of this landed in step 7**, which turned both store halves off in the flow adapter. So the store was inert for Steward runs from step 7 until here — nothing indexing, nothing reused — where before it was flow workers appending rows on flow's `write=True` default. That is the right way round: those rows were claims that a log is a valid result, made at the moment a log landed, which is before any scan, adjudication, or signoff. This step is what makes the store non-empty again, on Steward's terms and for every definition type rather than only for flow.

**The read half works here for a reason it could not work in a worker, and it was checked rather than assumed.** `reconcile._spawn_order` queues only `MISSING` and `INCOMPLETE` tasks, so a log copied into `logs/` leaves its task `COMPLETE` and nothing is ever started for it. That is precisely what execution.md §5.3 calls *broken* about flow's own read half — selection mode deliberately skips `eval_set()`'s reuse logic, so its workers run the selected task regardless and a copied log bought nothing while N of them raced onto one destination. Same mechanism, opposite outcome, and the difference is entirely which process is holding it. The launch's rung 2 sits immediately after the archive restore, keeping every `log_dir` mutation contiguous, and a store that will not open costs one recorded line and a task that runs the ordinary way.

**Both halves, both targets, on a dispatch rule with no ambiguous case: flow if and only if the location already carries a `flow_store/` marker.** execution.md §5.4 had recorded the directory implementation as a note — "the better answer if it is reached before step 33 needs one" — and it was reached. `observe_logs` was already the directory reader, since it groups *any* directory by `task_identifier` and says so in its own docstring, so the second implementation cost a quality rule and a copy. Three consequences were worth the rule: Steward never *creates* a table, so the `[flow]` extra is required exactly where somebody ran `flow store import` and the dependency wrinkle is removed rather than explained; the marker is checked without importing flow, a one-line duplication taken deliberately because deciding which implementation to use must not require the implementation; and **withdrawal splits without diverging**, since a table drops the row where a directory *moves* its copy into `withdrawn/` — `logs-archive/`'s bargain one level out, and out of reach for free because a store is read flat.

**Two decisions were the user's, and the second changed a design document rather than being implemented around it.** A task signed *with* accepted exceptions is published like any other — a signature is a signature, and withholding a result somebody explicitly accepted would make the store lie about what a project produced; what it costs is that the caveat does not travel, and the runbook says so at the moment the question is asked. And **publication has no default and no key at all**: execution.md §5.6 had it "defaulting from `_steward.yaml`", which misses who else is in the room — a store is frequently shared, so a key that can say `true` is publication nobody was asked about, fired by a command about something else from a file written months ago. So `--publish` is the whole surface, and the *ask* is the mechanism: `signoff_ready` names the configured store and tells the agent to put the question, the runbook carries it beside everything else prepared before the person is asked, and a signoff that published nothing while a store sat configured says so on its way out — the last moment anybody is looking, since the timer comes down immediately after and a signed run never tends again.

**A review round, five findings, and three of them were one habit: a claim defended by an argument nobody checked.**

*Publication ran before terminal verification.* `_publish` sat immediately after the signature, which reads correct — publication follows attestation — and is one step too early. The terminal finalize compacts scan rows for the first time where every earlier fold failed, and a window it uncovers *un-signs* the run: the verb returns blockers and reports that nothing was signed. So logs went into a shared cache and the operator was then told the run was not signed, which is exactly the export-of-unexamined-results that §5.5 exists to prevent, reached through the one door left open. It is behind both early returns now, last of the acts that leave the project.

*The directory's withdrawal did nothing*, on the argument that `search` ranks by quality so a superseded copy would be outranked by its replacement. It is not: quality is completed samples before recency, so a revoked four-sample result beats the two-sample one that supersedes it — and the replacement is short *because* a person accepted a hole in it. Withdrawal is a **move** into `withdrawn/` now, on `logs-archive/`'s bargain, and out of reach for free because a store is read flat. The argument was written to justify a decision already taken and was never checked against the ranking rule ten lines away.

*Store failures escaped as themselves.* Every caller promises to degrade a store failure to a warning, and every method normalized only `OSError`. fsspec backends raise their own hierarchies — `botocore.exceptions.NoCredentialsError` for an S3 store on a machine with no credentials, which is an ordinary Tuesday and was reproduced in one line — so the promise was broken at the two worst moments available: after a launch commits its manifest, and after a signoff records its signature. `except Exception` at the store boundary is the correct instrument here rather than a shrug, and it is argued for in the module docstring rather than left to look like one.

The other two were smaller and the same shape. *A copied log was claimed without being checked against the manifest*: `task_identifier` deliberately excludes the sample count, the epochs and the selection, so the store can hand back a log for the same identifier that ran a different slice — copied in, it leaves the task `INCOMPLETE`, the next tend queues it, and the delta has already said the work does not run here. It is filtered now through `answers_shape`, the predicate the archive restore already applies for the identical reason, plus the count that shape is blind to. And *`--publish` with no store configured succeeded in silence*, because the "nothing was published" line was suppressed on the grounds that publication had been asked for — the two silences were not distinguished, and the one that is a failure now warns and names the likeliest cause.

**A second round, six findings, and the through-line this time was a *second* copy of a rule that already existed.**

*Reuse eligibility disagreed with reconciliation.* The store filter checked `answers_shape` plus `completed_samples`, assembled from the same ingredients as observation's own predicate and not from the same code. It drifted exactly where a near copy drifts: observation compares `total_samples` and also weighs status and invalidation, so a full-count `status="error"` log was claimed as reused and then queued by the next tend, while a signed log carrying samples somebody accepted as errored was published by signoff and refused by the launch. `incomplete_reason` is public now and `observe_tasks` calls it too, so there is one definition of *complete* and nothing can hold a second.

*A near-miss masked valid candidates.* `search` returned the single best log, and the caller's check turned that into a veto: the store ranks by size and recency because a manifest-blind index can do nothing else, so its first pick may be the log that ran a different slice while the one behind it matches exactly. Both implementations return ordered candidates now — free for the directory, and free for the table too, since upstream already hands back what it did not pick as `duplicate_logs`.

*Publication included orphaned logs.* `_publish` read `current` off the pre-curation observation, and `plan` archives every attempt of an orphan **including its current one**, because nothing belonging to an identifier the definition no longer names belongs in the directory a signature covers. So a signoff published results the attestation excluded — and where the move had already landed, failed partway through the batch on a path that was no longer there, having already copied some of the valid logs. The set is narrowed by both things the function knows exactly: a manifest row, and not among what curation just moved.

*Withdrawing the same log twice left it reusable.* Publish, withdraw, publish, withdraw — what a re-signoff over a re-published directory does — and the second withdrawal met its own earlier copy in `withdrawn/` and declined to move, leaving the *newly republished* copy at the searchable root. Withdrawn twice and gone neither time. Collision-suffixed now, exactly as `logs-archive/` handles two attempts that reused a timestamp.

*A withdrawal failure erased a successful publication.* One `try` around both, so a store that took the logs and then would not drop the rows reported "nothing was published", wrote no journal event, and returned `None` — about a store holding every new log. Reported apart now: the publication is what the signature is about and is recorded when it lands; a failed withdrawal is a tidy-up left undone, which warns and says what it leaves behind.

*And `log_store: auto` was taken literally*, opening `./auto` — a **relative** directory, so one setting named a different store for every working directory a command was run from. It resolves to the machine's own store now, which is flow's data directory named as a path convention rather than reached through an import: the same trade `FLOW_STORE_MARKER` already makes, and for the same reason. A default that could not see the table `flow store import` builds would be worse than useless on the machine `auto` is for.

**A third round, five findings, and the through-line was a promise the code could not keep.** Each of the three sharpest is a sentence that reads as a design decision and had nothing behind it.

*Withdrawal was gated on `--publish`.* The store reached publication as `store if publish else None`, which made *taking a superseded result out* conditional on somebody asking to *put new results in* — and those are not the same permission. Publication exports this project's results and is the decision that gets prompted; withdrawal removes rows **this project itself wrote** for attempts it has just archived, exports nothing, and is nobody's decision to defer. So a signoff that published last month and this month curated that attempt away without the flag left the store handing out the log the project had just replaced, for as long as nobody typed it. The two are separated now: publication is still gated, withdrawal runs whenever a store is configured and something is owed to it, and a signoff owing nothing does not open the store at all.

*A failed withdrawal was called "the next signoff's problem".* There was no next signoff that could find it: curation has already moved the log to `logs-archive/` and `plan` works from what `logs/` holds, so the debt was outside every later run's reach and the warning that named it scrolled past once. The sentence described a recovery that did not exist — the shape being **a mechanism asserted in prose and never built**. It is a journalled ledger now, a per-store snapshot folded newest-first exactly as `read_smoked` folds a rehearsal, retried by every later signoff and closed out with an empty list when it clears. Keyed on the store, so repointing a workspace does not hand one store's debt to another.

*A directory publication announced partial success as its opposite.* The copies are sequential and nothing wraps them, so a batch that stopped partway had already put logs where a reader would find them — and raising on the first bad copy discarded that count, leaving the caller to report *nothing was published* about a store holding all but the last few, and to journal nothing at all. The distinction an implementation has to draw is between *took nothing* and *took some of it*; only the first is a `StoreError` now, and the rest come back in `Published.failed` for the caller to name.

The other two were narrower and both were a boundary drawn one call too early. *Relative store paths were resolved against the caller's cwd* — only `auto` was normalized, so `log_store: ../shared` was a different store to a launch typed at the root than to a signoff typed inside `logs/`: one setting, two stores, and reuse that quietly stopped finding what publication had put there. `store_location` resolves against the **workspace root**, which is the thing the setting is written down in and does not move. And *provider exceptions escaped after the store search*: `StoreError` covers the query because `_store` normalizes its own failures and covers nothing after it, yet what follows — reading a candidate's header, copying it in — are two more operations on the same backend, guarded against `OSError` and `ValueError`. The same `NoCredentialsError` the previous round chased out of the store was still reachable one frame further out, out of a launch that had already committed its manifest.

**A fourth round, three findings, and the through-line was an identity that was never established.** Each one turns on the same missing question: *is this the same thing?*

*A withdrawal was not tied to the publication that created it.* `curated.moved` was withdrawn from whatever `log_store` said today, which is not where the row is — and the two directions this fails in point opposite ways. Repointing a workspace from A to B stranded A's row permanently, since nothing would ever ask A about it again. And a directory store matches on the log's own filename, so a project that **reused** a log from a shared store and later archived that attempt moved the *producer's* copy into `withdrawn/` — ending reuse for everybody, over a log it had never published. The journal is the authority now: publication records what it wrote and where, withdrawal folds it, and each store is asked only about its own rows. It needs no publisher field to be a provenance record — it is *this* workspace's journal, so everything in it was written here; what it lacked was the log names, which the count could never supply. The distinction that closes the reuse case is in the store: `Published.written` reports what a call actually *put* there, which for a directory excludes a name that was already taken.

*Reuse did not account for workers already running the task.* The wanted set excluded logs and restores and not the fleet. A worker's log exists from the moment it starts, so the observation covers everything — except the pre-boundary window, where a process has been spawned and has no log and no control socket. An identifier in that window was indistinguishable from one nothing had ever run, so a relaunch copied a store result, reported the work as satisfied and not running here, and left the worker to spend money on it for as long as it took. `InFlight.running_identifiers` already existed and `reconcile` already refuses to queue a running task from it; rung 2 was simply not consulting it. A worker being stopped *outright* is carved out, since relocation and reshaping restart its tasks from nothing and that is where reuse is worth most.

*An interrupted copy was later reported as a success.* Both copy paths wrote straight to the final path and read the final name as proof of a finished log — `if not fs.exists(target)`. A truncated file therefore satisfied the check: publication reported `count=1, failed=()` while `observe_logs` could not read it and `search` answered nothing, and the launch path recorded a task as satisfied from the store by a file no reader could open. Reproduced in a dozen lines before it was touched. `copy_log` stages every write under a `.part` name and renames it into place, so a reader sees the log or nothing; and it verifies an existing destination against the source by size rather than trusting the name. The name-identifies-the-log reasoning was right and had quietly assumed the file under it was whole.

## 8. Hawk in the pod — after ship

[hawk.md](hawk.md) §11 stages Hawk in three, and only the third is here.

**Stage 0** — read and run a Hawk config — is done. **Stage 1** — Hawk on an ordinary machine, with the full workspace, tend loop, anomalies and signoff — is not separate work: a Hawk config is a definition type, so it falls out of steps 1–33. Its one Hawk-specific obligation is **step 7**, pulled to the front of execution for exactly that reason, plus two local caveats with no design content: `isolation: strict` hard-fails without `HAWK_RUNNER_PATCH_SANDBOX`, which only the Helm template sets, and `scan:` is rejected locally, so steps 25–27 have no Hawk path until this group.

**Stage 2 — Steward inside the pod — lands after ship.** It is architecture rather than configuration, it is the one stage needing a change on someone else's roadmap, and the three stages before it de-risk it. Nothing above waits on it.

### Step 35 — Blocking launch and exit codes

**Delivers** `steward launch --wait-signoff`: a process that holds the pod open for the whole lifecycle Steward defines.

- **Scope.** Workers as ordinary children rather than detached, since the runner is PID 1 and detaching buys nothing; the in-pod timer as a second driver of the same `reconcile`, sharing the run claim with an external tend; the exit-code mapping.
- **Refs.** hawk §7, §7.1, §8; exec §11.3.
- **Done when** the mapping is exercised end to end, including the non-obvious row: **terminal without signoff exits 0**, because a non-zero exit trips `backoffLimit` and the restarted runner resurrects the eval.
- **Also settles** hawk §12 q2 — how long a parked run waits before its deadline fires, and what the timeout writes.

### Step 36 — The relay surface

**Delivers** driving a pod-resident Steward from outside.

- **Scope.** A loopback TCP server inside the pod — Inspect's control channel cannot be borrowed, since the bind is hardcoded `AF_UNIX` with a PID-derived path and a `SO_PEERCRED` check, and `acp_server`'s `int → TCP 127.0.0.1:<port>` path is the precedent. A `steward --remote` client shaped by the relay's limits: **one connection per command**, because a five-session-per-principal cap and a 900-second idle timeout that keepalives do not reset both punish a pooled client. Recording a relay signoff as claimed-but-unverified, and declining to add a shared-token scheme below a real gate.
- **Refs.** hawk §9, §9.1, §9.2.
- **Done when** a full tend cycle runs over `hawk attach` without approaching either limit.

### Step 37 — The Hawk call site ⚠ Hawk-side

**Delivers** Hawk invoking Steward instead of `eval_set_from_config`.

- **Scope.** A runner type or flag at the one call site in `run_eval_set.py`. Not Steward's code.
- **Refs.** hawk §11; config §8.3.
- **Also settles** hawk §12 q4 — whether a resumed Hawk job is the same Steward run, which decides whether the journal survives a pod restart at all.

## 9. Why this order

Seven ordering choices are load-bearing. The rest of the sequence is just dependencies.

**`reconcile` before any process exists (5 before 6).** It is the component most likely to be subtly wrong and the cheapest to test exhaustively. Building the fleet first would mean debugging scheduling logic by watching it.

**Test infrastructure ahead of its subject (4 before 5, 11 before the recovery work).** The fixture generator is what makes `reconcile` a table instead of a fixture suite, and the fault harness lands at the first point where all the recovery claims have machinery behind them — so it grows with each later step rather than being retrofitted onto all of them at once. Both read backwards. Both are the reason the steps after them are cheap.

**Once-per-run ownership as a general step, not a Hawk one (7).** Flow and Hawk hit the same wall from opposite sides — one wastefully, one unsafely — and the ask is one mechanism. Filing it under Hawk is how it ends up implemented twice, so it sits in execution beside the spawn it constrains.

**Launch last in its group (16, after tend and the timer).** It is a composition of both, not a peer of either.

**Standing settings before the loop that reads them (12, moved up from after launch).** The tend interval, the non-convergence threshold, and the digest cadence are all standing properties of a workspace, and building the loop first would mean inventing a stub home for each and replacing it. The generalization is worth stating because it recurs: **when several steps need somewhere to put a decision, the somewhere goes first.** The file then grows one key per step rather than shipping a schema of keys nothing reads.

**Concurrency tuning split across the M2 gate (9 and 21).** The mechanism — the control channel client — is a wire protocol testable against one live worker, and `pause` and adjudication need it anyway, so it lands early in execution. The policy — signal, envelope, ratchet — needs the tend summary to carry the signal, so it lands immediately after step 19. Nothing breaks without the policy; the run is only slower, which is why the M2 gate does not wait for it.

**Notification before adjudication (24 before 25).** Anomalies need somewhere to escalate, notification is independently testable, and the channel's shape constrains the lifecycle.

**The runbook last before ship (34).** Argued in §7 above. It is the one step placed by an argument about *how design happens* rather than by a dependency.

Three steps are placed by external dependency rather than by design, and each is called out where it appears: **sandbox division (27)** would sit across steps 5 and 6 if upstream items 9 and 10 existed, **smoke (32)** would sit beside launch if item 8 did, and **worker startup at scale (18)** waited on item 5, which it landed itself — though half of it, the measured projection, is placed by a real dependency on launch.

## 10. The test budget

Thirty-seven steps each adding "just a few end-to-end tests" is how a suite reaches twenty minutes, and by then no one runs it before pushing. Step 1 measured what the cost actually is, so the rest of the plan can be held to a number instead of an intention.

**The unit of cost is the process launch, not the task and not the sample.** Measured on this machine:

| | |
|---|---|
| bare interpreter | 0.03s |
| `import inspect_ai` | 1.3s |
| capture — 2 tasks / 15 tasks | 2.50s / **2.31s** |
| one worker — 2 tasks / 15 tasks | 3.18s / **3.43s** |
| four workers, concurrently | 3.32s |

Fifteen tasks capture *faster* than two; thirteen extra tasks in a worker cost a quarter-second; four concurrent workers cost what one does. **Roughly 3s per launch, and the eval inside it is free.** Half the 3s is importing inspect_ai, which no amount of fixture care will avoid.

Three rules follow, and they are the opposite of the instinct:

1. **Count launches, not tests.** Step 1 runs five non-network cases in 33s because it launches eleven processes. Cutting a *test* saves nothing if it does not cut a launch; folding two assertions into one run saves 3s every time.
2. **Put more into each definition, not more definitions.** Step 1's dimensional fixture covers fifteen identity fields in one worker. Splitting it into fifteen focused tests would have been the same coverage for 45× the cost, and the shared-name construction that makes a dropped field collide is only possible *because* they share a run.
3. **Reach for a subprocess only when the process boundary is the subject.** [testing.md](testing.md) §1's layer 1 — `reconcile` over a synthesized log directory — is microseconds, and it is where most of this plan's correctness lives.

**Which steps genuinely need real workers**: 1 (done), 6–9, 11, 17, 20, and parts of 13, 16, 25, and 28–30 — call it twelve. Step 16 spent two of its twelve, which is what a seam at the capture buys; step 21 spent one, on the launch that watches a ramp actually climb. Steps 2–5, 10, 12, 14–15, 18–19, 21–24, 26–27, and 31–33 are layer 1 or near it: synthesized state, pure functions, and at most a launch apiece. **Budget ~12 launches for a layer-2 step**, which is roughly 35s serial and under 10s with `-n auto`. Ten such steps lands the whole suite near five minutes serial and one to two minutes on CI. That is the line; a step that wants more should say why in its design pass.

**Running total after step 17**: 728 offline tests, 50s with `-n auto`. Forty-two tests and **one launch**, for a step that changed the shape of every worker.

**Running total after step 18**: 735 offline tests, 60s with `-n auto`. Seven tests and **no new launches**, on the Steward side — the step's substance is upstream (seventeen cases, 8s in inspect_ai's suite), and Steward's half is two fields in a document and a number on a manifest. The rule held in an unusual way: the *claim* to be tested belonged to another repository, so the budget question was not how many launches to spend but which repository to spend them in.

**The review pass found two real defects, and both were the same defect.** A packed worker's live rows are named by correlating `TaskRow.log_location` against the observation, and a packed worker's *partial* stop is decided the same way — so both paths take a `locations` mapping that the default width never reads. `_tend/turn.py` called `read_fleet` without one, which cost every task of a packed worker its identity and reported all of them `finished`; `_worker/stop.py` counted its `LEFT` outcome as graceful, so a partial stop that could not correlate reported success while the task went on running. The first is now structural rather than fixed: **`locations` is a required positional argument**, because a caller that omits it is only ever wrong — unread at width 1, silently destructive above it. The second is a one-word change to `Stop.graceful` plus the offline `stop_workers` tests that should have existed, since a *safe* failure that reports itself as success is worse than one that raises.

**And the reviewer's third P1 dissolved rather than being fixed.** `[tool.uv.sources]` pointed at a local inspect_ai checkout because the v4 selection override was unmerged; it merged during the step, so the source went back to git main and the lockfile with it. The related claim — that changing what `max_workers` means needs a migration — has no object: Steward has never been released, so no workspace anywhere carries the old meaning. What did need fixing was the documentation that still taught it, and the `_steward.yaml` and `runbook.md` **templates** were the important half, since `steward init` writes them into every new workspace.

**The pour is pure, so the interesting half cost nothing.** Every combination of the two knobs against pending work, tasks already in flight, and processes already alive is one table over a four-line function — eleven rows, no filesystem, no processes. That is the same property that made `reconcile` cheap to test and it compounded here: the tests that already existed for the ceiling became tests for `max_tasks` by changing the argument, because the *decision* layer never learned what a process is.

**The one launch buys a claim nothing else can make**, and it is not the one it looks like. That both logs land from a single process is worth a process to check, but the assertion that pays for the test is `config.max_tasks == 2` in the landed logs — the whole chain from a `_steward.yaml` key, through the pour, into a selection document, across a process boundary, and into the record inspect writes. A faked capture cannot reach past the boundary, and the failure it guards against is silent: a batch that runs one task at a time still lands every log, just slowly.

**Two regression guards are the unusual thing here, and they are guarding an absence.** The stem and `STEWARD_TASK` were made byte-identical at width 1 rather than merely equivalent, so the tests assert the literal old shapes — `k_{sha256(id)[:8]}_1` and a bare identifier. Without them a later refactor could "tidy" the one-element case into something reasonable-looking and break the process-table scan for every existing workspace, which is a fault that shows up as a live worker being respawned over itself rather than as a failing assertion.

**A second review pass found four more, and every one of them was width-1 thinking surviving into a packed world.** They are worth listing together because the pattern is the lesson: code that was *correct* at one task per process became *wrong* rather than merely incomplete, and in three of the four cases it went on producing a plausible number.

- **The queue named the wrong knob.** `_cli/turn.py` inferred the binding limit from which key was *set*, so a run with `max_workers: 1` and `max_tasks: 10` and its one process alive told its operator to raise `max_tasks` — a number that would have changed nothing. Only the pour knows which bound held, so it now says: `pour` returns `Poured(workers, queued, blocked)` and the renderer reads the answer instead of guessing it. At width 1 the two questions genuinely were the same question, which is why the inference looked sound when it was written.
- **The live read was serial in the batch.** `_read` awaited one `/samples` request per task in a dict comprehension. That is one round trip at the default width and five hundred for a one-process sweep — the serial fleet read this module was created to avoid, rebuilt inside a single worker, and not bounded by `timeout` either, since that bounds each request. Now one `asyncio.gather` over the distinct evals.
- **The connection pool was a process fact presented as a task fact.** `_connections` sums every adaptive controller in the process, which *is* the task's pool when the process holds one task, roles included. Packed across four models it gave every row all four pools added together. Now narrowed to the row's own model above width 1 and left summing everything at width 1, so the roles case keeps working. Two residues that narrowing cannot remove, because they are true of the process: a controller shared by two packed tasks on one model appears in both rows, and a packed task's role models are dropped since nothing in the row names them.
- **`--max-samples` still said "per worker".** It is per task, and under packing the difference is multiplicative — `max_samples: 40` on a worker holding ten tasks is four hundred concurrent samples, not forty. A help string, but the wrong one to get wrong.

**The fifth finding is real and is not a bug, and the distinction matters.** `resolve_pool` reads `None` as *no preference, defer to `_steward.yaml`*, which is what every other flag in the CLI does and what an unsupplied flag should do. What it costs is that a workspace setting either key cannot be widened back to unbounded for one turn, because `int | None` has two states and the question has three. Fixing that means a sentinel — `--max-workers all` — reaching through `tend`, `status`, `launch`, and `resolve_pool`. Left for the user to call, with the docstrings that claimed otherwise corrected in the meantime.

**Running total after step 16**: 686 offline tests, 46s with `-n auto`. Thirty-six tests and **two launches**, for the step that starts runs — which is rule 1 and rule 3 doing the work together, and one new application of the seam idea.

**The capture is a seam, and treating it as one is what kept this cheap.** `launch` must read the definition, and reading a definition is a subprocess costing seconds. Every shell-contract case — the gate both ways, the journal entry, the arming, the re-launch argument defaulting, the scanner refusal, the credential refusal, the subdirectory cwd, `--json` — fakes `read_eval_set` and is real on both sides of it: real delta, real manifest commit, real archive, real journal, real first turn. Twenty-six cases in 1.3s. The real capture is exercised where it belongs, in the one live test that also runs workers, and the fake computes its content hash from the definition on disk so that a launch's own first turn reports `drift is False` exactly as a real one does.

**The fake's `calls` list is what makes a pre-capture refusal assertable**, and that is worth naming as a pattern. *The credentials check happens before the capture* is a claim about ordering, and the only honest way to state it is that the capture was never called — `assert capture.calls == []`. A test that merely checked the message would pass just as well against an implementation that spent five minutes on a Hawk config first.

**A live test moved up rather than being added to.** `test_tend_live.py` already performed launch's four steps by hand, which meant the composition a person actually runs was the one thing never exercised against a real eval. Rewriting it to go through `launch` bought the commit, the arming, the journal entry, and the delta for the *same three launches* — plus one extra capture and no extra workers for the claim that two captures of one unedited file agree about every identifier.

**One thing had to be shared rather than copied a third time.** Clearing the ambient credentials before arming was a fixture body in two test modules and about to become three; it is now `clear_credentials` in `tests/timer/_fake.py`, scoped by the production predicate so a variable the check learns to recognise is a variable it clears. The related trap: `launch` has no `--scheduler` flag, so which backend these tests arm depends on the machine — no case names one, and all three are safe because every backend reaches the system through the faked `run_command` and the two that write files write them under `Path.home()`, which the autouse fixture has already moved.

**And the 54s figure below was never a slowdown, which is worth recording since two runs were spent on it.** After repointing `[tool.uv.sources]` at a local inspect_ai checkout the suite appeared to take 240s. It had not changed: the earlier figure was `-m "not network"` and the new one was not, and the two hawk network tests — which `uv pip install` on every invocation — accounted for nearly all of the difference on their own. (248s of it on a cold uv cache; 13s on a warm one, which is worth recording because *the hawk tests cost 200s* is not a stable fact and only ever was one about a cold cache.) **A running total measured under one marker selection is not comparable to one measured under another**, and this file's totals are all offline.

**Running total after step 15**: 650 offline tests, 54s with `-n auto`. A hundred and seventy-nine tests and **no launches**, which is the seam doing all the work. A scheduler backend is two halves: **rendering** a plist, a systemd unit, or a crontab line is a pure function and is tested exhaustively, while **activating** it mutates the developer's machine and is never run — every backend takes a `Runner`, and a test asserts the argv it built. What that seam buys is that the cron block edit, which is the genuinely dangerous code here, gets a dozen cases for nothing.

**The step's launch count went to zero by deleting code, not by weakening tests, and that is the more interesting fact.** It originally spent about nine launches, all of them on the ticker: a detached process of Steward's own was the one backend it owned end to end, so a fake would have been testing the fake, and *turns happen that nobody typed* cannot be asserted without a real one running real tends. Removing the ticker removed both the backend and the only claim in the step that needed a process. What replaced it costs nothing: the CLI tests drive **cron with an in-memory crontab** (`tests/timer/_fake.py`), which is the one system backend whose whole interface is *read the file, write the file* — so faking it is faking a file rather than a scheduler, and an `arm` can still be followed by asking whether it took. Everything else is left to a manual pass, which is what caught the two things no unit test would have: launchd installed and firing on this machine, and the spurious `timer_drift` on a one-off `--interval`.

**One test moved down a layer during that pass, and the reason generalizes.** The `.env` credential check has a case — `KEY=` with nothing after it must not satisfy it — that reads naturally as a CLI test and cannot be one: `init_dotenv()` overrides existing variables when it detects a VS Code terminal, so an empty `.env` entry clobbers the shell's real key *before* the check runs, and the same test passes or fails depending on where the developer typed `pytest`. It belongs against `unavailable_credentials`, where the two environments are arguments. **A test whose answer depends on the terminal it was started from is not a flaky test, it is a test at the wrong layer.**

**Running total after step 14**: 462 offline tests, 63s with `-n auto`. Forty-seven tests and **no launches**, for the step that owns what a turn says — which is rule 3 at its most literal, since a rendering takes a value and returns a string. The one place it needed care is that an item is a *projection*, so building `Item`s by hand would have tested the constructor: every case here synthesizes the condition in a workspace — a definition edited after capture, a file that is not a log, three logs that got exactly as far as each other — and asserts on what came out of a real `tend`. The stall cases take the log-visible route rather than the crash loop, because the crash loop needs processes and this file is not about the guard.

**Running total after step 13a**: 415 offline tests, 54s with `-n auto`. Forty-nine tests and **no launches**, for a step whose whole subject is talking to running processes. Two of the three files are rule 3 again — the cache is a listing against a dict, the rows are a dataclass against a synthesized log directory — and the third is the fourth category in a form the rules did not have: **a real server that is not a worker**. `read_fleet` exists because of a timing claim, so a stubbed client would test the parsing and discard the part under test; an `asyncio` AF_UNIX listener in a thread costs nothing, speaks real HTTP over a real socket, and can do the one thing no eval will do on demand — accept a connection and never answer. The two tests that turn on that pay 0.25s each, which is the timeout they are asserting.

**That category has a cost the other three do not, found during step 15: a hand-rolled server must be honest about the protocol it claims to speak.** This one answered `HTTP/1.1` and then closed each connection without sending `Connection: close` — so the client pooled a socket the server had already dropped, and the second of `_read`'s three requests died on EOF as a `RemoteProtocolError`, reporting a healthy worker as `gone`. Almost never when the machine was idle, better than one run in three under a full `-n auto` load, and indistinguishable from a real timing bug in the code under test. **A fake that speaks a protocol approximately produces flakes shaped exactly like the bugs it was built to catch**, which is worse than a stub would have been — so the honesty of the fake is part of the cost of choosing this category, not a detail of it.

**Running total after step 13**: 366 offline tests, 50s with `-n auto`. Step 13 is the largest step so far — the turn, two CLI verbs, three new actions — and added ninety-one tests for **three launches**, at no measurable cost in wall time. Rule 3 did nearly all of it: the turn takes a workspace, and a workspace is a directory, so nineteen of those tests drive the real `tend` against a hand-committed manifest and a log directory `tests/_logs.py` wrote.

The one thing that could not be faked is the crash loop that lands no log, since the point of it is that the log directory shows nothing — and that is the fourth category again, at its cheapest yet: **the definition is `# a definition that exits`**. A worker spawns, the interpreter starts and stops, and the in-flight record is the only trace, which is precisely the state under test. Five of them, ~30ms each, against real `Fleet.spawn`, real `resolve_inflight`, and the real guard. The alternative was mocking the fleet, which would have tested the mock.

That left one launch group worth paying for, and it buys the claim no fixture can make: **that the layers agree about the same run.** Capture writes identifiers and a content hash, two workers write logs, observation matches the two, and a third turn does nothing — one capture and two workers, in one test, asserting six things. The live *stall* test the design pass had budgeted was dropped rather than written: it would have re-run a path already covered with real processes, in exchange for learning that `faulty_evalset.py`'s `pre:crash` lands no log, which step 11 already establishes.

**Running total after step 12**: 275 offline tests, 45s with `-n auto`. Step 12 added forty-six tests and **no launches at all** — the whole step is a parser, a refusal table, and a precedence chain, which is rule 3 in its purest form. The 2.4s it cost is fixture overhead rather than work, and the one test that touches the filesystem meaningfully is the one asserting that the template `init` ships actually parses as front matter, which is the failure that would otherwise break every workspace at once. Step 11 added five tests and three launches; the seven seconds it cost are roughly what the migrations gave back, since the tests it absorbed had been paying fixed sleeps to keep an eval alive. Step 10 added nineteen tests and no evals at all, for 7s serial and no measurable change in parallel. Step 9 added sixteen tests, one launch, and about ten seconds — nearly all of it in `inspect ctl` invocations rather than in evals, which is a cost category the budget below did not have. Step 6 was the first layer-2 step since step 1 and came in at 12 launches, exactly the budget — but eight of those are *relocated* rather than new, because the two step-1 selection tests that ran the production shape moved onto the real spawn instead of being duplicated beside it. Step 7 added one test and three launches, step 8 eighteen tests and three. Wall time has moved by about three seconds across all three, which is rule 1 working.

Step 8 is the clearest case yet for rule 3: the scan is a parameter, so twelve of its eighteen cases are a table over a stubbed one, and the three launches are spent only where the process boundary genuinely is the subject — a worker held in the window before its eval, one killed inside it, and one that finishes. Its one deliberate cost is a five-second sleep in the gated fixture, bought because a socket that exists for less time than it takes to look for it cannot be asserted on.

It also found a fourth category the rules did not name: **a real process that is not an eval**. Proving that a worker's children are not the worker needs actual parent-and-child processes and no eval whatsoever, and `python -c "time.sleep(120)"` costs about 50ms. Four of them appear here. The rule is worth stating — *when the process boundary is the subject but the eval is not, do not launch an eval* — because the instinct is to reach for the fixture that already exists.

Step 10 put a price on that category, and it has two tiers. Its five claim holders each import `inspect_steward` and so cost **1.3s** rather than 50ms, because `inspect_steward/__init__` reaches `inspect_ai`; the alternative — a child writing the claim payload with plain stdlib — was declined, since a test that hand-writes the format it asserts on is testing itself. Its seven *bystanders* exist only to be a live pid that a hand-written payload can point at, import nothing, and cost the full 50ms discount. The rule: pay the import only when the child has to run Steward's own code. Under `-n auto` the whole step is free either way.

Step 9 added a fifth, which sits between the two: **an `inspect ctl` invocation costs ~1.3s**, all of it importing inspect_ai, so it is a tenth of a launch and two orders of magnitude more than a function call. Two consequences fell out of that number rather than out of taste. A live test must not spend invocations casually — the one here folds polling and pid-filtering into a single call — and **the eval has to outlast them**, which is why the gated fixture's sleep became settable. The other half of the step is table-driven over a `_decode` split out from the subprocess, which costs nothing and covers the outcomes no test can manufacture: a wedged event loop, a malformed body, an error kind this version has never heard of.

One thing the budget did not anticipate: **the two measurements step 7 took are not tests and should not become them.** Timing a flow worker against a baseline, and racing two `uv pip install` to see whether uv locks, each answered a question the design had been guessing at — and each would be a slow, flaky, third-party-dependent test if pinned. Measure, write the number in the design with its date and method, move on.

**Levers held in reserve**, in the order they become worth their complexity: cache captures across tests in a session (deterministic by the contract in configuration.md §4, so it is safe — but xdist gives each worker its own cache, so it pays off only once a single xdist worker runs many tests); share one worker run across several assertions; and, last, split the suite so layer 2 runs on a different cadence than layer 1.

## 11. What this plan does not decide

- **The internals of any step.** Each gets a design pass, starting from its **Refs** line.
- **Sizing.** [roadmap.md](roadmap.md) §3 declines to attempt dates and this document does too.
- **Where ship falls** between the M3 gate and step 34. Only that it falls before §8.
- **The open questions.** Twenty-odd remain across the docs, distributed over the steps that own them. None blocks step 1.
- **The one real gap.** Nothing surfaces an agent's mistake ([roadmap.md](roadmap.md) §7). It belongs to step 23 and is not yet solved there.
